Abide With Me — one of the most-requested funeral hymns

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 2 May 2026 · Updated 25 October 2026

Abide With Me and The Lord’s My Shepherd are the two hymns families ask us for most at funerals. People choose it whether or not the person who died was religious, in Norman parish churches and in 1970s crematorium chapels, for fifteen mourners or three hundred. British congregations have sung it at funerals for more than a century.

Who wrote Abide With Me, and why is it sung at funerals? Henry Francis Lyte, the minister at Lower Brixham in Devon, wrote the words in 1847 while dying of tuberculosis; he died in Nice that November. Families choose it for a funeral because it asks God to stay close at the end of a life, and its tune, Eventide, sits low enough for a congregation that rarely sings.

The words of Abide With Me

Lyte wrote eight verses in 1847, and the text is out of copyright. Hymn books usually print five of them, and most funerals sing those. The wording below follows the 1920 Army and Navy Hymnal; other books vary slightly in punctuation and in “thou” or “Thou”.

Abide with me; fast falls the eventide;
The darkness deepens; Lord, with me abide.
When other helpers fail and comforts flee,
Help of the helpless, O abide with me.

Swift to its close ebbs out life’s little day;
Earth’s joys grow dim, its glories pass away;
Change and decay in all around I see;
O thou who changest not, abide with me.

I need thy presence every passing hour;
What but thy grace can foil the tempter’s power?
Who like thyself my guide and stay can be?
Through cloud and sunshine, O abide with me.

I fear no foe, with thee at hand to bless;
Ills have no weight, and tears no bitterness.
Where is death’s sting? Where, grave, thy victory?
I triumph still, if thou abide with me.

Hold thou thy cross before my closing eyes;
Shine through the gloom, and point me to the skies.
Heaven’s morning breaks, and earth’s vain shadows flee;
In life, in death, O Lord, abide with me.

A family that wants a shorter hymn can keep the first, second and last verses. Tell us which verses you want and we will sing those; our funeral choir prices start at £250 for a soloist.

The words were written by a dying man

Henry Francis Lyte moved to Lower Brixham, a Devon fishing town, in 1824 and served its church for the rest of his life. By 1847 he had tuberculosis. His daughter’s account says he preached to his congregation one last time in September 1847 and gave the finished hymn to a relative that evening; his biographers now think he wrote it a few weeks earlier, in July or August. He travelled south for his health and died in Nice, then part of the Kingdom of Sardinia, on 20 November 1847.

Lyte knew he was dying when he wrote “Swift to its close ebbs out life’s little day”, and a congregation at a funeral can hear it. He writes from inside the fear the mourners in front of the coffin are facing, and he offers them no easy comfort.

The last line, “In life, in death, O Lord, abide with me”, asks for company. Stay close; don’t leave. A family wants that on the day of a funeral, and Lyte puts it in plain words.

Sources: “Henry Francis Lyte” and “Abide with Me”, Wikipedia.

The melody is built for grief

William Henry Monk wrote the tune, Eventide, for the first edition of Hymns Ancient and Modern in 1861; he was the book’s music editor. Hymn historians repeat, with caution, a story that he wrote it in ten minutes at a committee meeting after he noticed that Lyte’s text had no tune.

A grieving congregation can sing this tune without strain. It sits in the middle of the voice, with no high note to reach for, and moves in even steps. Someone who has not sung a hymn in twenty years can find it by the end of the first line. Someone in tears can mouth the words and keep their place with the people beside them.

An organist or choir can hold the early verses back and open up the last, so the congregation has a firm lead when the singing matters most to them.

Source: “Eventide”, Hymnary.org.

It works in any setting

We sing Abide With Me in parish churches, college chapels and crematorium chapels. It needs no grand acoustic. A single soloist singing the first verse unaccompanied can hold a small chapel of rest in silence.

At Anglican and Free Church funerals it is a standard choice. At a Catholic funeral it fits as a hymn outside the Mass, usually after the eulogy or as the coffin is brought in. Families planning a non-religious or humanist service sometimes ask for it anyway, because Lyte’s words dwell on presence and love more than on doctrine. If the family wants it, we would not try to talk them out of it.

Abide With Me has been sung before the FA Cup Final since 1927, when Arsenal played Cardiff City; four years earlier a church choir had sung it during a delay at the 1923 final. The Rugby League Challenge Cup Final has kept the same custom since 1929. The hymn was sung at the burial of the Unknown Warrior in Westminster Abbey in 1920 and at the state funeral of George V in 1936, and Emeli Sandé sang it at the opening ceremony of the London 2012 Olympics. Many people who would not call themselves churchgoers know it from Wembley, and they reach for it when they need a hymn for someone they loved.

Source: “Abide with Me”, Wikipedia.

What it sounds like with a choir

With only an organ, a congregation can lose its way when voices falter. A choir holds the line, keeps the tempo steady and adds harmony under the tune, so the people in the pews have something to lean on.

We arrange the verses to build. Verse one in unison over a quiet organ; verse two in two parts; verse three in four-part harmony; the last verse in full voice, with the congregation singing more confidently than they expected to. In Greater London a soloist costs £250, a soloist with an organist £500 and a choir of four £1,150, with travel added outside London. Where to place it in the service

We suggest Abide With Me as the closing hymn. Lyte wrote about facing the end of a life with company, and many families want to leave the service on that note. Sing it last, with the choir in full voice, and the congregation leaves with a tune they can hold on to.

If the family wants two or three hymns, Abide With Me sits well in second or third place. Open with something more pastoral — The Lord’s My Shepherd, or All Things Bright and Beautiful for someone who loved gardens and the countryside — and save Abide With Me for later, when the congregation has settled.

It pairs with music the congregation listens to. After a soloist sings Schubert’s Ave Maria, Abide With Me brings everyone back to singing together. After a Bach chorale or a piece by Fauré, it gives the room a tune they know. What families tell us

Families ask us two questions about Abide With Me more than any others: will the congregation manage it, and how many verses should we sing? A congregation will manage it if someone leads, which is the reason to book a choir or a confident soloist. If the crematorium slot is short, we can cut a verse; keep the first and the last.

Frequently asked questions

Who wrote Abide With Me?

Henry Francis Lyte, minister of All Saints’ Church in Lower Brixham, Devon, in 1847. He was dying of tuberculosis; the traditional account says he finished the hymn on the evening of his last sermon in September, though his biographers now date it to July or August. He died in Nice on 20 November 1847.

What tune is Abide With Me sung to?

Eventide, written by William Henry Monk for the first edition of Hymns Ancient and Modern in 1861. The melody sits in the middle of the voice with no large leaps, so a congregation that rarely sings hymns can join in from the first verse.

Can Abide With Me be sung at a Catholic or non-religious funeral?

Yes. At a Catholic funeral it fits as a hymn outside the Mass, usually after the eulogy or as the coffin is brought in. Families planning a non-religious or humanist service sometimes ask for it anyway, because the words dwell on presence more than doctrine.

Why is Abide With Me associated with the FA Cup Final?

Crowds have sung it before the FA Cup Final since 1927, when Arsenal played Cardiff City, and before the Rugby League Challenge Cup Final since 1929. Many people who know few other hymns learned this one at Wembley.

Where can I find the words of Abide With Me?

Lyte’s text is out of copyright and printed in Hymns Ancient and Modern and most other English hymn books. For a funeral, the order of service usually prints the verses the family has chosen; tell us which verses you want and we will sing those.

Who has recorded Abide With Me?

Audrey Assad closes her 2016 album Inheritance with it, and Emeli Sandé sang it at the London 2012 Olympics opening ceremony. Thelonious Monk recorded a jazz arrangement in 1957. Cathedral and college choirs have recorded it many times. At a funeral, a live soloist or choir can lead the congregation in it.

How much does it cost to have Abide With Me sung at a funeral?

In Greater London a soloist costs £250, a soloist with an organist £500, and a choir of four £1,150, which covers rehearsal and the service. We add travel for funerals outside London.
