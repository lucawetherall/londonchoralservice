How many carol singers do you need?

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Last updated: July 2026

The honest answer is that it depends on the ceiling more than the guest list. A room with a hard floor and height above it carries four voices further than a carpeted function suite carries eight. This guide works through the sizes we book most, what each one covers, and the point at which adding voices stops making a difference.

Start with the room, not the headcount

Most people size a booking by the number of guests. It is the wrong first question. Two hundred people in a church with a stone floor and a high vaulted roof need fewer singers than a hundred people in a hotel function room with carpet, low ceilings and soft furnishings, because the church does half the work and the function room fights you.

What matters is how much of the sound comes back. Hard surfaces reflect it; carpet, curtains and full bookshelves absorb it. Height gives the sound somewhere to bloom before it reaches anyone. When we ask for photographs of a venue before quoting, this is what we are looking at, along with where the bar is and which direction the chatter will be coming from.

The second question is what the singers are for. A group leading congregational carols needs the strength to be heard over a room already singing, which is a different requirement from a group performing to a seated audience. Leading takes more voices than performing does.

Why four is the smallest unaccompanied group

Carols are written in four parts: soprano, alto, tenor and bass. With four singers you have one voice on each line, which is the smallest group that can sing the harmony as written without leaving a hole in it.

Take one away and something has to give. Three voices means dropping a part or asking someone to jump between two, and the gap is usually at the bottom, which is where the sound gets its warmth. A congregation will not be able to name what is missing, but they will sing more tentatively, because the bass line is what tells people where the harmony is going.

This is why our carol bookings start with a Small Choir of four. It is not a minimum-order rule; it is the smallest group that sounds like a choir rather than like some singers.

Four singers

Four voices in full harmony will lead a room of about 150 through Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, fill an office reception or a hotel lobby, and carry a village-scale church. This is the most common Christmas booking we take, and for most events it is the right answer.

What four singers cannot do is split. The bigger carol arrangements divide into six or eight parts, and a quartet has to sing a reduction of those instead. If your programme is built around the Willcocks descants or double-choir writing, four is where you start feeling the limit.

A Small Choir of four is £1,150, inclusive of taxes, fees and travel within Greater London.

Six singers

Six gives you two voices doubling on the outer parts, usually the sopranos and basses. The practical effect is that the group punches through background noise: a busy hotel foyer with a bar running, a mid-sized hall, an outdoor switch-on with a crowd talking through the countdown.

Six is the size we suggest when the room is loud rather than large. Four unamplified voices can lose a fight with fifty people holding drinks; six generally win it. A Sextet is £1,600.

Eight singers

Eight covers a large church or a full carol service with congregational hymns, and can split into eight parts for the bigger anthems. If you are booking a church that seats 400 and expect it full, this is the size that will lead the singing rather than accompany it.

Eight also gives the programme more range. Pieces that need a proper choral sound rather than a consort sound — Howells, Darke, the double-choir carols — open up here. A Full Choir of eight is £2,000.

Twelve singers

Twelve is for cathedrals, ticketed concerts, and congregations of several hundred. At this size the choir stops being a group at the front and becomes the sound of the building. If you are selling tickets on the strength of the music, twelve is what people are picturing when they buy them.

A Chorus of twelve is £3,000. Above twelve we quote per project.

When two or three voices work

There is one case where fewer than four makes sense: when there is an organ or piano underneath. An instrument covers the harmony the missing voices would have carried, so two or three singers over an organ can hold a chapel service that the same two or three would struggle with unaccompanied.

Priced from the standard rates, that is £250 per singer plus £225 for the organist, so £725 for two voices and organ, £975 for three. A single voice works on its own terms for a sung grace at a dinner or one set piece, at £250.

What we will not do is send two or three singers to stand in a lobby with no instrument. It does not work, and December is not the month to find that out.

What else changes the answer

Outdoors. Open air takes the reflections away, so you lose the help the building was giving you. Add two voices to whatever the indoor answer would have been.

Roaming versus stationary. Singers moving through a crowd are only ever heard by the part of the room they are standing in, which means a smaller group can work harder. Singers performing a set to the whole room at once need to reach the back of it.

Whether the room sings. If you want a singalong on the last three carols, size up. Leading is harder than performing.

Amplification. We do not normally use it, and trained voices rarely need it. Where a venue is hostile — a shopping centre with music playing over the top — we will say so rather than send a group that cannot be heard.

Sizing by venue type

Office reception or atrium

Four. Office atriums are usually hard-surfaced with height, which is the best acoustic a building can give you for free. Put the singers at the foot of a staircase or on a mezzanine and the sound reaches three floors. The only reason to go to six is if the reception runs alongside a bar and a hundred people arriving at once.

Hotel lobby or festive afternoon tea

Four for a tea service, six for an evening lobby. Afternoon tea rooms are quieter and the singers are working close to seated guests; an evening lobby has a bar, a concierge desk, luggage, and constant movement through it. Six voices hold that without anyone pushing their voice, which matters across a three-set evening.

Church carol service

Four for a church seating up to about 200, eight for one seating 400. The deciding factor is how full it will be: an empty church is a resonant one, and a full church is not, because people absorb sound. If you are expecting the building full and the congregation singing, size to the congregation rather than the architecture.

Livery hall or gala dinner

Four for a sung grace and a short set. Livery halls are panelled, which absorbs more than stone, but the room is usually silent when the singing starts, so you are not fighting anything. Six if the choir is performing between courses while service continues around them.

Apartment lobby or residents’ evening

Four. These events are usually forty to eighty residents with mulled wine, and the point is a singalong rather than a recital. Four voices and a song sheet does more here than eight voices and a programme.

Christmas market or light switch-on

Six at least, eight if there is a PA playing anything nearby. Outdoors removes the reflections, and a crowd outdoors is louder than the same crowd indoors. This is the one setting where under-booking is obvious to everyone present.

Three sizing mistakes worth avoiding

Sizing to the guest list. A hundred people in a carpeted function suite is a harder room than three hundred in a church. The building decides more than the headcount does.

Booking a large group for a roaming set. Singers moving through a crowd are only heard by the part of the room they are in. Twelve voices roaming is twelve voices being heard by thirty people at a time, which is an expensive way to do what four would.

Forgetting the room fills up. Venues are quoted empty and experienced full. Coats, bodies and a carpet of conversation take a surprising amount of sound out of a room, which is why we ask what time the singing starts relative to the drinks.

If you are not sure

Send us the venue, the date and a rough guest count, and say whether you want the room singing or listening. We will tell you which size fits and, where the smaller one would do, we will say that too. Sizing up is not always the answer, and quoting for voices a room does not need is a quick way to lose a client for next year.

Photographs help more than dimensions. What we are looking for is the floor, the ceiling height, and where the noise will be coming from. If you have a floor plan from the venue, that is better still.

Frequently asked questions

Is four carol singers enough for a carol service?

For most carol services, yes. Four voices in full harmony will lead a congregation of around 150 and cover the standard carol repertoire. If you are expecting 400 in a large church, or want the choir to split into eight parts for the bigger anthems, book eight.

Can you book just two carol singers?

Only with an organist or pianist. Two voices cannot hold four-part harmony on their own, so unaccompanied groups start at four. Two singers with an organ is £725, priced at £250 per singer plus £225 for the organist.

Do more carol singers mean a wider repertoire?

Yes, from six upwards. Four singers cover the standard carols but must sing a reduction of anything written in more than four parts. Six adds weight on the outer lines, and eight can split into eight parts for double-choir pieces and the fuller descant arrangements.

How many carol singers fit in a hotel lobby?

Four suits most lobbies, and six if there is a bar running and a crowd talking over it. The singers need somewhere to stand together rather than a stage, and a hard floor with height above it carries the sound further than any extra voices would.
