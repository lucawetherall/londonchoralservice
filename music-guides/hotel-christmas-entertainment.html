Christmas entertainment for hotels

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Last updated: July 2026

A hotel at Christmas has something almost no other venue has: a building already full of people. Guests come down for afternoon tea, drop bags at the desk, wait for a table, stand about with a glass before dinner. Live carol singers turn those in-between minutes into the part of the stay guests film and put online. And they ask very little of the building — no stage, no power, no sound check. This guide covers which format suits a lobby, a tea room, and a ballroom, how to schedule singers across December, what the choir wears and sings, and what to settle before you sign.

Why live carol singers work in a hotel

Most festive budgets go on things guests look at: the tree in the atrium, the garlands up the staircase, the window display. Live singing is the only part of that spend guests stop for. Four voices starting Silent Night in a marble lobby empty the lift queue and refill it thirty seconds later with people holding phones up. Those clips go onto guest accounts with your hotel tagged, which is a return the garlands do not produce.

The practical case is shorter. A professional choir singing a cappella needs a patch of floor and somewhere to leave coats. Nothing gets plugged in, no cable crosses a guest route, no engineer has to be booked, and there is no sound check to schedule around lunch service. Trained voices carry across a lobby or a restaurant on their own; that is what the training is for. If your lounge has a piano and you want it used, we can send a pianist too, though the singing does not depend on one.

One thing does need checking: the room itself. Hard surfaces flatter unaccompanied voices, so a stone stair, a glass atrium, or the back wall of a bar all help the sound reach the far end. A low lounge with deep carpet and a full sitting in progress absorbs a great deal, and there you want six voices rather than four, or the singers moved to where the ceiling is highest. Describe the space when you enquire and we will tell you the number.

Formats by space

The same singers can work four quite different rooms in a hotel, and the format changes with each one.

The lobby

Two approaches. Roaming means the group moves through the space in a loose knot, sings three or four carols in one spot, then relocates — by the tree, at the foot of the stairs, near the concierge desk. That suits the arrivals peak, when the audience changes every few minutes and nobody stays long enough for a full set. Stationary means the singers hold one position and the room comes to them, which suits the drinks hour once people have settled. Plenty of hotels use both inside one booking: roaming from four o’clock, stationary once the bar fills.

Festive afternoon tea

Afternoon tea wants background singing, and background singing wants gaps. Twenty minutes on, twenty minutes off, repeated across the sitting, lets tables talk between sets and gives every seating in the room something rather than loading it all onto the first hour. Where the tea room has a piano, the usual booking is choir with background piano between sets: the pianist covers the gaps so the room is never silent and the singers still get their breaks. Where there is no piano, we can bring a keyboard and speaker. That carries an extra charge, and it appears in the written quote before you commit.

Restaurant and ballroom

A private dining room or a ballroom party is a performance job rather than a background one. The singers stand as a group, the room turns to face them, and the set runs fifteen to twenty-five minutes: two or three unaccompanied carols, an arrangement of something the guests know from the radio, then a singalong finale on Hark! The Herald Angels Sing or O Come, All Ye Faithful. The singalong is what makes a corporate table of forty behave like a party rather than a dinner. Print the words on the menu card or hand out song sheets and you will get several times the voices.

Frontage, terraces, and light switch-ons

The choir can sing outdoors. A switch-on at the front door, carols on a terrace with mulled wine, singers on the steps as a coach party arrives — all workable, and unamplified voices hold up in open air when the crowd is gathered round rather than spread across a square. Cold is the limiting factor rather than the acoustic: the singers need somewhere warm to wait, and outdoor sets run shorter for that reason. For a switch-on, send us the countdown time so the carol lands on the moment the lights come up.

Scheduling across the season

Two shapes of booking cover most hotels. A one-off evening — the staff party, a residents’ drinks, the switch-on — is a single date with a single agreed format. A recurring booking puts the same singers in the building on several December dates: every Saturday of festive afternoon tea, say, or the three Fridays before Christmas. The recurring version is worth raising early. It fixes the dates in one conversation, and it gives you the same faces each time, which matters once your duty manager has briefed them on where to stand and when to stop for the speeches.

A standard Christmas booking is up to an hour and a half of the choir’s time, including their breaks. Inside that you can have one long set or three short ones, and the shape is yours to choose. Programmes that run longer are perfectly possible; they carry a premium, quoted upfront in writing rather than added to the invoice afterwards. If you are hiring the choir into a church service instead, the booking is measured by the length of the service.

What the singers wear

Dress should follow the room rather than the season, and you choose it. Concert dress — long black, black tie — suits a ballroom, a gala dinner, or a formal afternoon tea. All black without the formality suits a lobby set that should read as part of the building. Casual suits a family switch-on or a market. Christmas jumpers are available if the evening wants them, and we carry Santa hats to go with any of the above. Period costume is the one thing we do not offer. Tell us what your own front-of-house team is wearing that night and we will pitch it to match.

Repertoire for a mixed guest list

The repertoire is the traditional carol book plus a cappella arrangements of the popular Christmas songs. On the carol side, the singers know O Come, All Ye Faithful, In the Bleak Midwinter, Silent Night, the Sussex Carol, and Ding Dong Merrily on High by heart. On the other side sit close-harmony versions of Have Yourself a Merry Little Christmas, White Christmas, and Winter Wonderland.

That balance matters more in a hotel than almost anywhere else, because your guest list is not all British and not all churchgoing. A couple visiting from Tokyo for the shopping may never have heard Once in Royal David’s City; they will recognise White Christmas within two bars. Tell us roughly who is in the building that week — a corporate block booking, half-term families, an international leisure mix — and we weight the running order accordingly. Sheet music, rehearsal, and the programme are handled at our end.

Practical booking notes

December fills from the top. Our December diary closes out by mid-September, and the Fridays and Saturdays of the second and third weeks go first. Read this in the summer and you are choosing your date; ring in November and you are choosing from what is left. A late enquiry is still worth making, because cancellations happen, but the range of ensemble sizes narrows quickly.

Prices are the standard rates on our pricing page and do not change for Christmas: a Small Choir of four from £1,150, a quintet from £1,400, a sextet from £1,600, a full choir of eight from £2,000, and a twelve-voice chorus from £3,000. A pianist or organist is from £250. Every figure includes taxes, fees, and travel within Greater London. The one Christmas premium falls on 24 and 25 December, and it is confirmed in the written quote before you commit.

Frequently asked questions

What Christmas entertainment works in a hotel lobby?

Live carol singers, in one of two shapes. Roaming: the group moves between the tree, the stairs, and the concierge desk across the arrivals peak. Stationary: the singers hold one position and guests gather round. Both work unamplified, so nothing blocks a guest route and the front desk keeps working through it.

Do carol singers need a stage or sound system?

No. A professional choir sings a cappella, so the booking needs a patch of floor and somewhere to leave coats. There is no staging, no power, no speakers, no sound check. If you want piano as well and the hotel has no instrument, we can bring a keyboard and speaker for an extra charge, quoted upfront.

How much does hotel Christmas entertainment cost?

A Small Choir of four singers starts from £1,150, a sextet from £1,600, a full choir of eight from £2,000, and a pianist or organist from £250. Those prices include all taxes, fees, and travel within Greater London, and they do not rise at Christmas. Only 24 and 25 December carry a premium, confirmed in your written quote.

Can the same choir return across several December dates?

Yes, and hotels often book that way: the same singers on every Saturday of festive afternoon tea, or on the three Fridays before Christmas. Booking the dates together fixes them before the diary fills and means the group already knows your room and your timings. Ask early, because December closes out by mid-September.
