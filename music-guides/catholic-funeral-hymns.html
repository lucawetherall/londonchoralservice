Best hymns for a Catholic funeral

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 6 March 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

A Catholic funeral Mass follows the Order of Christian Funerals, and its music belongs at fixed points: the entrance, the psalm, the preparation of the gifts, Communion, the farewell and the procession out. This guide goes through those points, then the hymns and solo pieces families ask us for most, and what to agree with the parish priest.

What are the most popular hymns for a Catholic funeral? Nobody keeps an official count. At the Requiem Masses we sing, families ask most for The Lord’s My Shepherd (Crimond), Be Not Afraid, Here I Am, Lord, Make Me a Channel of Your Peace and Abide with Me. The solo we are asked for most is Schubert’s Ave Maria at Communion, and older families still choose Soul of My Saviour.

How music fits into a Catholic funeral Mass

A Requiem Mass is the Catholic funeral liturgy, set out in the Order of Christian Funerals that the bishops of England and Wales published in 1990. It has fixed points for music, and choosing for each point is easier than fitting favourite pieces in wherever they will go.

The reception and entrance. The priest meets the coffin at the church door and sprinkles it with holy water. The congregation then sings an opening hymn as the coffin is carried to the front. Choose something strong and well known: people are least ready to sing at this point.

The Responsorial Psalm. After the first reading a cantor sings the verses of a psalm, and the congregation answers each one with a short response. Psalm 23 (The Lord Is My Shepherd) is the usual choice; the funeral readings offer others, Psalm 27 (The Lord Is My Light and My Salvation) among them.

The Gospel Acclamation. An Alleluia before the Gospel, with a different acclamation in Lent. The cantor leads it.

The preparation of the gifts. A hymn or a choral piece while the altar is prepared. A gentler choice suits this point, after the readings and the homily.

The Mass setting. The Holy, Holy, the Memorial Acclamation, the Great Amen and the Lamb of God are sung parts of the Mass itself. Most parishes have a setting their people know, and a cantor or choir leads it.

Communion. The distribution takes several minutes, long enough for a solo or a choral piece. Ave Maria and Panis Angelicus are the traditional choices.

The Final Commendation. The priest sprinkles and incenses the coffin and commends the person to God. The rite gives this moment a Song of Farewell for everyone to sing, often Saints of God, come to his aid, an English version of the Latin Subvenite, or a hymn in its place.

The procession out. As the coffin leaves, the rite gives the antiphon In paradisum (May the angels lead you into paradise), sung in Latin or English. Many parishes sing a hymn here instead, something steady and hopeful the congregation can stand and sing.

The hymns families choose most

Be Not Afraid

Bob Dufford, one of the St Louis Jesuits, wrote it in 1975; the verses draw on Isaiah 43 and the Beatitudes in Luke’s Gospel. Many Catholic congregations know it well, which helps when people are too upset to follow the words on the page. It works as the entrance hymn or the last hymn, and it is a good first choice for a family unsure where to start.

Here I Am, Lord

Dan Schutte wrote it in 1979, on the call of Isaiah and of the boy Samuel. The verses are quiet and the chorus opens out, so it suits the preparation of the gifts or the last hymn. Families who grew up in Catholic parishes usually know it, and it sings well with a small congregation.

Ave Maria (Schubert)

Schubert wrote the melody in 1825 as Ellens dritter Gesang, a setting of Walter Scott’s The Lady of the Lake in German. The Latin Hail Mary was fitted to it later, and that is the version sung at Mass. It is a solo, and the one we are asked for most at Catholic funerals. It suits Communion, or a moment after the readings, with organ or piano, and guests who are not Catholic know it from the first bar. You can hear our soloists on our listening page.

Panis Angelicus (Franck)

The words are a verse of Sacris solemniis, Thomas Aquinas’s hymn for the feast of Corpus Christi. They speak of the Eucharist, which makes this the natural Communion piece. César Franck set them in 1872 for tenor, harp, cello and organ, and later added the piece to his Messe à trois voix. At a funeral a tenor or soprano usually sings it with organ.

How Great Thou Art

Carl Boberg wrote the Swedish original in 1885, and Stuart K. Hine made the English version in 1949. Catholic and Protestant churches both sing it. The refrain is broad, so it suits the entrance or the last hymn when you want the congregation to sing out, and most people can manage the refrain without the book.

The Lord’s My Shepherd (Crimond)

A metrical Psalm 23 from the Scottish Psalter of 1650, sung to the tune Crimond. It is the hymn we are asked for most at funerals of every kind, and most of a congregation can sing it without the book. It works as a hymn at any point. Some priests accept it in place of the Responsorial Psalm; others want the psalm from the Lectionary with its response, so ask. Our guide to popular funeral hymns has more on this and the other well-known choices.

Make Me a Channel of Your Peace

Sebastian Temple, a South African member of the Franciscan Third Order, wrote it in 1967 on the Prayer of St Francis. The prayer first appeared, unsigned, in a French Catholic magazine in 1912 and was later attached to the saint’s name. The tune is gentle and easy to sing. It suits the preparation of the gifts or the last hymn, and families choose it for someone they remember for their kindness.

On Eagle’s Wings

Michael Joncas, an American priest, wrote it in 1976 on Psalm 91. The verses are quiet and the refrain opens out into a wide, warm melody. It suits the entrance or the preparation of the gifts, and families who know it from Sunday Mass ask for it by name.

Soul of My Saviour

An English version of the fourteenth-century prayer Anima Christi, sung to a tune by the Jesuit William Maher (1823–1877). Many older Catholics know it from school and Sunday Mass. Fewer people know it outside Catholic churches, so it marks the music as the family’s own tradition. The words are a prayer after Communion, and Communion is where it belongs.

Anima Christi (Frisina)

The Latin original of Soul of My Saviour, in the setting by Monsignor Marco Frisina, a priest of the Diocese of Rome. Its closing petition, in hora mortis meae voca me (in the hour of my death, call me), is why families ask for it at a Requiem. It sits at Communion or at the Final Commendation. At a funeral the choir or a soloist usually sings it while the congregation listens.

Abide with Me

Henry Francis Lyte wrote the words in 1847, the year he died, and the hymn is sung to William Henry Monk’s tune Eventide (1861). Catholic families choose it as often as anyone, most often as the last hymn. A strong lead from the singers carries a congregation through the final verse, when voices tend to fall away.

Choosing from the Catholic repertoire means matching each hymn to its point in the Mass. We do this with families at churches across London and beyond.

The Responsorial Psalm

The cantor sings the response first, so everyone hears it, then sings each verse of the psalm, and the congregation repeats the response after each one. Psalm 23 is the usual choice for its words of comfort. Psalm 27 is more about trust and courage, and suits a family that wants the tone confident rather than consoling.

A trained cantor makes the psalm easier for everyone. The verses come across clearly, and the congregation knows when to come in. Our singers lead the psalm at funeral Masses often, and they take it at an unhurried pace.

Working with the parish

The parish priest decides what music is sung at a funeral Mass. The Church asks that the texts express the Christian hope of the resurrection and relate to the readings, and many parishes keep secular songs out of the Mass altogether. The parish of Harpenden, in the Diocese of Westminster, says non-religious music is best kept for the crematorium or the graveside. Some priests allow quiet instrumental music. Ask before you print the order of service.

If you book outside musicians, let us talk to the parish directly. The parish organist may want to accompany the hymns, and the priest may prefer a particular psalm or Mass setting. Settled a week ahead, none of this is difficult. Our guide to hiring a choir covers how this works in practice, and our funeral music costs guide sets out the prices.

Frequently asked questions

What hymns are sung at a Catholic funeral?

Families often choose The Lord’s My Shepherd, Be Not Afraid, Here I Am, Lord, How Great Thou Art, Make Me a Channel of Your Peace, On Eagle’s Wings, Soul of My Saviour and Abide with Me, with Schubert’s Ave Maria or Franck’s Panis Angelicus sung as a solo at Communion.

What is the most popular Catholic funeral hymn?

No official figures exist. At the funeral Masses we sing, the hymn families ask for most is The Lord’s My Shepherd, sung to Crimond, and the most requested solo is Schubert’s Ave Maria at Communion.

What are the traditional Catholic hymns for a funeral?

Soul of My Saviour, Panis Angelicus and the Ave Maria are the long-standing Catholic choices, with chant from the old Latin Requiem such as In paradisum. Be Not Afraid, Here I Am, Lord and On Eagle’s Wings are newer, written in the 1970s, though many families now treat them as traditional.

Can you have non-religious music at a Catholic funeral?

Usually not during the Mass. The parish priest decides, and many parishes keep secular songs out of the liturgy and suggest them for the crematorium, the graveside or the wake. Some priests allow quiet instrumental music. Ask the parish before you print the order of service.

What is sung at the end of a Catholic funeral?

At the Final Commendation everyone sings a Song of Farewell. As the coffin leaves, the rite gives the antiphon In paradisum (May the angels lead you into paradise), in Latin or English. Many parishes sing a hymn here instead, such as Abide with Me or How Great Thou Art.
