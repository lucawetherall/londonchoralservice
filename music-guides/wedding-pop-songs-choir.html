Pop songs for a wedding choir to sing

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 16 April 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

Take away a pop song’s drums, synths and studio production and a good one keeps its tune, its harmony and its words, which is all four voices in a church need. This guide covers pop songs sung by a choir: the ones our singers are asked for at weddings, where each sits in the ceremony, and what makes a song survive the move to four parts. For pop songs played on the organ alone, see our guide to pop songs on the organ; for the classical repertoire, see the best choral pieces for a wedding.

What pop songs can a choir sing at a wedding? The pop songs couples ask our choirs for most are A Thousand Years (Christina Perri, 2011), All of Me (John Legend, 2013), Perfect (Ed Sheeran, 2017), Can’t Help Falling in Love (Elvis Presley, 1961) and Stand By Me (Ben E. King, 1961). Almost any song with a strong tune can be arranged for four voices, sung unaccompanied or with an organ.

We provide choirs for weddings across London and the rest of the UK. A Small Choir of four singers costs £1,150, or £1,400 with an organist, for up to two hours, rehearsal included, in Greater London.

Modern love ballads

Most couples who ask for a pop song in their wedding want one of these. You will have heard them at other weddings and on film soundtracks, and the songwriting is good enough to stand the repetition.

A Thousand Years (Christina Perri, 2011)

The pop song our choirs are asked to sing most often. Christina Perri and David Hodges wrote it for The Twilight Saga: Breaking Dawn – Part 1. A four-part a cappella arrangement passes the melody between the voices and gives the sopranos the top line in the final chorus. The original runs just under five minutes, enough to cover the signing of the register.

All of Me (John Legend, 2013)

John Legend wrote it with Toby Gad for Chrissy Teigen, whom he married in 2013, which is its own argument for singing it at a wedding. The piano part of the original moves into vocal harmony without trouble, and the chorus lies well for a mixed choir. A solo voice over three-part backing suits it.

Perfect (Ed Sheeran, 2017)

Simple harmony, simple rhythm, and a melody every guest knows. It rewards a straight, unfussy arrangement more than a clever one. A tenor soloist with a small choir on the chorus gives you the intimacy of the original plus the warmth of voices in a church.

Make You Feel My Love (Bob Dylan, 1997 / Adele, 2008)

Hymn-like in its structure, which is why it belongs in a church. Bob Dylan wrote it for Time Out of Mind, and Adele’s 2008 cover made it a wedding song. We like a soprano soloist with the choir entering on the second verse. Works during the signing of the register or as a post-vows piece.

Thinking Out Loud (Ed Sheeran, 2014)

Ed Sheeran wrote it. Its soul-leaning chords give a choir more to do than most pop songs of the last decade. Divide it into five or six parts and the inner voices can move independently against the melody, which makes the arrangement satisfying to sing and to hear.

Marry You (Bruno Mars, 2010)

Bruno Mars wrote it with Philip Lawrence and Ari Levine. It is upbeat and light on its feet, and the title does the job. Use it for the recessional: the rhythm carries the walk out even without drums.

Classic ballads

Older songs tend to translate best to voices because they were written in an era when melody and harmony carried the song on their own. If you want something that feels timeless rather than tied to a specific year, start here.

Songbird (Fleetwood Mac, 1977)

Christine McVie wrote Songbird in about half an hour one night and stayed up so she would not forget it. In March 1976 she recorded it alone at the piano in an empty Zellerbach Auditorium in Berkeley, and it came out on Rumours in 1977. It is close to a choral piece already. A small choir — four to eight voices — with the melody in the soprano keeps the stillness of the original. It suits the signing of the register.

Stand By Me (Ben E. King, 1961)

Ben E. King wrote it with Jerry Leiber and Mike Stoller over the classic doo-wop chord progression (I–vi–IV–V), which already sounds close to a hymn. A straightforward four-part arrangement works as a recessional if sung with energy, or a register-signing piece if taken slower.

Can’t Help Falling in Love (Elvis Presley, 1961)

Its songwriters, Hugo Peretti, Luigi Creatore and George David Weiss, borrowed the melody from an 18th-century French song, Martini’s “Plaisir d’amour”, which is why it feels like something older than it is. A men’s quartet, or a full choir with the melody in the tenors, suits it well. Most often chosen for the register signing.

Unchained Melody (Alex North and Hy Zaret, 1955)

Alex North wrote the music and Hy Zaret the words for Unchained, a 1955 prison film. The Righteous Brothers’ 1965 recording, with Bobby Hatfield singing the lead alone, made it a wedding standard. A soprano or tenor soloist over four-part backing suits the signing of the register.

Fields of Gold (Sting, 1993)

Sting wrote it about the barley fields near his home in Wiltshire. It has the folk simplicity of an English traditional song and the directness of a pop song. A four-part a cappella arrangement lets the lyric breathe. Best during the prelude or the signing of the register.

Upbeat pop for the recessional

The walk back down the aisle rewards energy. These songs work when you want your guests grinning from the first chord.

Signed, Sealed, Delivered, I’m Yours (Stevie Wonder, 1970)

Stevie Wonder wrote it with Syreeta Wright, Lee Garrett and his mother, Lula Mae Hardaway. It is joyful from the first bar, and a choir can carry the groove without drums because the harmonic language already does most of the rhythmic work. Suits a recessional better than anything else on this list.

Home (Edward Sharpe & The Magnetic Zeros, 2009)

A banjo-and-whistle folk-pop song that sounds at home in a church when sung by voices alone. The call-and-response structure suits a small choir splitting into two groups. Works as either a prelude or a recessional.

You Are the Best Thing (Ray LaMontagne, 2008)

A Stax/Motown soul feel that a good choir can reproduce without instruments. The chorus has the warmth of a gospel anthem, and it sends a congregation out of the church smiling. It is as joyful as the Stevie Wonder song, and fewer guests will expect it.

Here Comes the Sun (The Beatles, 1969)

George Harrison’s melody is constructed from clear, major-key intervals that sit well in four parts. A choir of eight or more gives the chorus the lift it deserves. Works either at the very start of the prelude or as a recessional.

What makes a pop song work for a choir

Ask three questions of any song you’re considering.

Is the melody strong on its own? Songs built around a distinctive vocal performance rather than a strong melody rarely survive the move to four-part harmony. Songs built around a clear, singable tune almost always do. The melody of A Thousand Years works because you can hum it. You often can’t hum the melody of a contemporary R&B song at all, because the singer’s ornaments carry it.

Is the harmonic structure interesting enough to arrange? A choir arrangement needs inner parts that have something to do. Songs with two or three chords on loop leave the alto and tenor with nothing to sing except parallel thirds. Songs with moving bass lines or surprising chord changes (Ed Sheeran, Stevie Wonder, older standards) give arrangers real material.

Does the lyric say something you actually want said? A church is an attentive room. Every word gets heard. Songs that read as cheesy or generic on paper will sound that way, so read the words through before you choose.

If you’re unsure about a song, we can read through it and send you an honest assessment before anything is committed.

Bespoke choral arrangements

There is no published four-part version of most pop songs, which means somebody has to write one. This is part of what The London Choral Service does; depending on the piece, a bespoke arrangement can carry a small extra charge, agreed upfront.

Our arrangements are written by Luca Wetherall, our Artistic Director, or one of our senior musicians. A typical arrangement takes one to two weeks to write, and we send you a scratch recording (one voice per part, recorded at the piano) so you can hear the shape of it before the rehearsal.

Changes are welcome at that stage. If the key is too high, we adjust. If the opening feels too sparse or the chorus too busy, we rework it. The point is for the music to sound like what you imagined, which usually takes one or two rounds of revision.

The choir rehearses the arrangement at least twice before the wedding day. On the day itself, the musicians arrive an hour before the ceremony to run through anything delicate with the venue and to confirm the final running order.

Where to place pop songs in a ceremony

The signing of the register is the most flexible slot. Five to eight minutes of uninterrupted attention, no movement, and guests already seated means almost any pop song works. This is where the A Thousand Years and All of Me requests usually end up.

The recessional wants rhythmic energy. Upbeat ballads and soul-leaning pop (Stand By Me, Signed, Sealed, Delivered, Here Comes the Sun) work well. A slow ballad struggles against the physical action of walking out of the church.

The prelude — the fifteen minutes as guests arrive — is the safest slot for anything unusual. Nobody is watching the altar, conversation is quiet, and a pop arrangement here becomes a pleasant backdrop rather than a centrepiece.

The processional is the hardest slot. A pop song has to sustain walking pace for a full minute or more, and most don’t. If you want a pop processional, we’ll usually write an extended intro or loop a section so the music matches the walk. Frequently asked questions

Can a choir sing any pop song at a wedding?

Most pop songs can be arranged for a wedding choir. Ballads with a strong tune and clear harmony work best. Heavily produced or drum-driven tracks are harder to move to four voices.

Can a church refuse a pop song at a wedding?

Yes. In the Church of England the parish minister has the final say on the music at a wedding (Canons B 20 and B 35), and most will discuss a pop song for the signing of the register or the way out. Catholic parishes are often stricter about secular songs. Send your list to the minister before we start an arrangement.

Do pop songs need copyright clearance for a wedding?

Not usually for the live music. PRS for Music, at its discretion, does not charge for music performed as part of a wedding ceremony, religious or civil. Filming or livestreaming the ceremony can need a separate licence, so check with the church or venue before you book a videographer.

How many singers do we need for a pop song arrangement?

Four singers, one to a part, are enough for most arrangements; our Small Choir of four costs £1,150 in Greater London. Eight voices give the chorus more weight and allow five- or six-part writing, and a choir of eight costs £2,000.

How long does it take to prepare a choral arrangement of a pop song?

A bespoke four-part arrangement typically takes one to two weeks to write. Allow the choir at least two rehearsals to learn it. Eight to twelve weeks from first enquiry is comfortable. Depending on the piece, an arrangement can carry a small extra charge, agreed upfront.
