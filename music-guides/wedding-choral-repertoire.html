The best choral pieces for a wedding

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 6 March 2026 · Updated 25 October 2026

At a wedding the choir leads the hymns, and it also sings on its own where the ceremony pauses: as guests arrive, during the signing of the register and after the vows. This guide covers the pieces that work best at each point, with composers and dates. For sizes, logistics and costs, see our guide to hiring a wedding choir.

What are the best choral pieces for a wedding? The standards are Schubert’s Ave Maria (1825) and the Bach/Gounod Ave Maria (1859), Franck’s Panis Angelicus (1872), Mozart’s Ave Verum Corpus (1791) and Laudate Dominum (1780), Fauré’s Pie Jesu, the Ubi Caritas of Duruflé (1960) or Ola Gjeilo (1999), and John Rutter’s The Lord Bless You and Keep You (1981).

Music as guests arrive

The prelude is the first music your guests hear, while they arrive and find their seats. Quiet unaccompanied pieces suit it, and soft voices tell guests before the ceremony starts that the music will be live. Our guide to wedding ceremony music sets out the whole order of service, from prelude to recessional.

Ubi Caritas (Duruflé)

Duruflé published this in 1960 as the first of his Quatre motets sur des thèmes grégoriens, built on the plainchant melody. The text, “Where charity and love are, God is there”, suits a wedding, and the harmony shifts gently under the chant without ever growing loud. It is unaccompanied and lasts about two minutes. A quartet or octet can sing it.

Ubi Caritas (Gjeilo)

Ola Gjeilo wrote his setting of the same text in 1999, while still a student, and it is now the version couples ask us for most. Where Duruflé stays quiet throughout, Gjeilo divides the voices and lets the harmony build to a full climax before settling again, which carries better to guests who do not otherwise listen to choral music. It runs about three minutes, a good length for a signing of around five, with the organ to cover any pause. See our fuller guide to Ubi Caritas at a wedding.

Ave Verum Corpus (Mozart)

Mozart wrote it in June 1791, six months before he died, for Anton Stoll, the choirmaster at Baden near Vienna. It is 46 bars long and takes about three minutes, with four voices moving together in slow chords. That makes it a good prelude: guests can follow it while they settle, and it sounds as well unaccompanied as with organ.

Locus Iste (Bruckner)

“This place was made by God.” Bruckner wrote his setting in 1869 for the dedication of a votive chapel in the new cathedral at Linz, so the words fit guests arriving at a church. The harmony is broad and warm, and the piece is unaccompanied and short. It needs four confident voices, and a resonant building helps it.

During the signing of the register

This is the choir’s longest slot. The couple and their witnesses sign the register, which usually takes five to ten minutes, and the music has the room to itself. You can choose pieces here for how they sound rather than for a place in the liturgy. These are the ones couples ask for most.

Ave Maria (Bach/Gounod or Schubert)

The piece couples request most at weddings. Schubert wrote the tune in 1825 as Ellens dritter Gesang, a song from Walter Scott’s The Lady of the Lake; the Latin prayer was fitted to it later. Gounod laid his own melody over the first prelude of Bach’s Well-Tempered Clavier and published it with the Ave Maria words in 1859. Schubert’s is the more intimate, a solo voice with a long arching line. The Bach/Gounod is more ornate, and a small choir can hum or sing beneath the soloist.

Panis Angelicus (Franck)

Franck wrote it in 1872 for tenor with organ, harp and cello, and added it to his Messe à trois voix. Most couples hear it as a solo with organ, or in an arrangement where the choir joins the soloist for the second verse. The words are by Thomas Aquinas, which makes it a traditional choice at Catholic weddings, though it suits any church ceremony. It sounds best with a tenor, but any voice can take the solo.

Pie Jesu (Fauré or Lloyd Webber)

Fauré’s comes from his Requiem, which he wrote and revised between 1887 and 1890: a soprano solo over organ, slow and quiet. Andrew Lloyd Webber’s, from his Requiem of 1985, was written as a duet for soprano and treble and has a broader, more lyrical tune. Both texts are a prayer for the dead. Some couples mind that and some don’t, so it is worth deciding before you print the order of service.

Laudate Dominum (Mozart)

From Mozart’s Vesperae solennes de confessore of 1780. A soprano sings Psalm 117 over a gentle accompaniment, and the choir enters for the closing Gloria. The solo line sits high enough to carry to the back of a church. It runs four to five minutes, which covers most register signings.

The Lord Bless You and Keep You (Rutter)

John Rutter set the Aaronic blessing from the Book of Numbers in 1981, for the memorial service of Edward Chapman, his old director of music at Highgate School. It has since become a standard at weddings and christenings. It is short and warm, written for choir and organ, and four or more voices give it body. Some couples place it during the signing; others keep it for the end of the service, as a blessing on the marriage.

O Mio Babbino Caro (Puccini)

Lauretta’s aria from Puccini’s one-act opera Gianni Schicchi (1918), in which she begs her father to let her marry the man she loves. It is a soprano showpiece that builds to a high climax and then falls away. It is a less traditional choice than the sacred pieces above, and some churches and couples find it too theatrical, so check with your minister first. Couples who want one moment of opera in the ceremony choose it.

After the vows

Some ceremonies have a musical moment after the vows or during communion. Couples usually pick something quieter here than for the register signing, often a blessing sung over the newly married pair.

An Irish Blessing (traditional words)

“May the road rise to meet you, may the wind be always at your back.” The words are traditional, and composers have set them many times; Bob Chilcott’s setting is one choirs know well. It is a wish for the couple’s future, short and warm in four parts, and it sits after the vows or as a closing piece. Our guide to pairing readings with music has more on placing these moments.

A Gaelic Blessing (Rutter)

“Deep peace of the running wave to you.” Rutter wrote it in 1978 for the Chancel Choir of First United Methodist Church in Omaha. He described the words as an old Gaelic rune; they come from a poem William Sharp published as Fiona Macleod in 1895. The harmonies are rich and the tune simple, for choir with organ. It suits a quiet moment after the vows, or the end of the ceremony as people prepare to leave.

Brother James’s Air

“The Lord’s my Shepherd” to the tune by James Leith Macbeth Bain, known as Brother James, usually sung in Gordon Jacob’s choral arrangement. The tune is gentler than the hymn-tune versions, and the choir sings it to the congregation instead of with them. Couples who love Psalm 23 but want something quieter than Crimond often choose it.

Contemporary and less traditional choices

Not every piece at a wedding needs to be sacred or classical. A favourite song arranged for voices can sit alongside the anthems, and a choir can sing it in a church without it sounding out of place. Our guide to pop songs for a wedding choir covers more of them.

A Thousand Years (Christina Perri, choral arr.)

Christina Perri released it in 2011, and it has become a frequent wedding request. In a four-part a cappella arrangement the voices pass the melody between them, and the song settles into a church acoustic. It works during the register signing or as a prelude piece.

Make You Feel My Love (Bob Dylan/Adele, choral arr.)

Bob Dylan recorded it in 1997, and Adele’s 2008 cover made it a wedding standard. The tune is plain and the lyric direct, so it arranges well for unaccompanied voices, and the words come through clearly.

Songbird (Fleetwood Mac, choral arr.)

Christine McVie wrote it for Rumours (1977). The lyric is a short love poem. A solo voice can carry it, or a small choir can add soft harmonies under the melody. It suits the register signing or a quiet moment after the readings.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best choral pieces for a wedding?

The standards are Schubert’s Ave Maria and the Bach/Gounod Ave Maria, Franck’s Panis Angelicus, Mozart’s Ave Verum Corpus and Laudate Dominum, Fauré’s Pie Jesu, Ubi Caritas by Duruflé or Ola Gjeilo, and John Rutter’s The Lord Bless You and Keep You.

What is the most popular choral wedding piece?

Schubert’s Ave Maria is the piece couples request most, usually during the signing of the register at a church wedding. At a civil ceremony the registrar will not approve the sung text, which is a prayer, so ask about an instrumental version instead.

Do these pieces need an organ?

Some do. Both settings of Ubi Caritas, Locus Iste and Mozart’s Ave Verum Corpus can be sung unaccompanied. Ave Maria, Panis Angelicus, Pie Jesu, Laudate Dominum and Rutter’s blessings need an organ or piano. An organist is £275, or £250 added to a choir.

How many choral pieces does a wedding need?

Two or three is usual: one as guests arrive, one or two during the signing of the register, and sometimes a blessing after the vows. The hymns come on top of these, with the choir leading the congregation.

Can a choir sing contemporary songs at a wedding?

Yes. We arrange pop songs for four or more voices, usually unaccompanied, so they suit a church acoustic. A Thousand Years and Make You Feel My Love are frequent requests.
