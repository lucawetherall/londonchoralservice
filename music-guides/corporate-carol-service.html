Planning a corporate or office carol service

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 4 March 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

A carol service gives a company something a drinks reception can’t: an hour in which the whole office, from new joiners to senior partners, sings the same carols in the same room. This guide covers the date, the venue, the music and readings, a sample running order and the costs, whether the service is for staff in your own atrium or for clients in a City church.

How do you plan a corporate or office carol service? Pick a Tuesday, Wednesday or Thursday in the second or third week of December, then a room: a church, your own atrium or a hotel function room. Book the singers by September. Choose six to eight carols and three to six readings, print the words in a programme, and allow 45 minutes at lunchtime or an hour after work.

Why a carol service works for businesses

The office Christmas party has its place. A carol service gives you an occasion with a fixed running time and no open bar, and among corporate event music options it is one of the easiest to hand to someone else to run.

For client-facing firms — law practices, financial advisers, property companies — it works as hospitality. Clients see the care that went into the music, the readings and the venue, and they remember the evening.

Internally, it puts staff from different floors and offices in one room doing the same thing. New joiners, senior partners and the post-room team all stand up together when O Come, All Ye Faithful starts.

It is also a manageable event to organise. You need no seating plan, no dietary-requirements spreadsheet and no playlist. Everyone knows the format, and the running time is predictable.

Step by step

1. Set a date

Lunchtime suits an office: a 12:30–1:15 slot fits inside the working day, and nobody has to rearrange an evening. An end-of-day service from 4:30 to 5:30, followed by drinks and mince pies, feels more festive and can double as the office Christmas gathering. A service for clients usually starts at 6pm and runs into a reception.

Aim for the second or third week of December. Earlier than that it feels premature, and in the last week before the break many people are already on leave. A Tuesday, Wednesday or Thursday tends to draw the best attendance.

2. Choose a venue

A church or chapel. A church is the natural home of a carol service. The building was designed for singing, and a stone church by candlelight at four in the afternoon in December gives you an atmosphere no function room can match. Many City of London churches host corporate carol services and have a set hire process. Guild churches, livery-company halls and the chapels of the Inns of Court are worth asking, and outside the City a large parish church near your office may welcome the booking. December is the busiest month in any church’s calendar, so ask early.

Your own offices. An atrium, a large boardroom or a reception area costs nothing to hire, everyone knows where it is, and the event feels like the company’s own. The trade-off is acoustics. Glass, carpet and low ceilings soak up unamplified singing, so a deadened office may need more singers than a church of the same size. Tell your musicians about the space in advance and they can advise on positioning, numbers and whether any amplification is needed.

A hotel or function room. This suits a large company hosting clients, where the service needs to run straight into drinks and canapés without moving everyone across town. Function rooms vary: a high-ceilinged ballroom with a hard floor sounds good, a low, carpeted conference suite much less so. Send the musicians photographs and a floor plan, or let them visit.

Whichever you choose, test the room before you commit. Clap your hands in it and listen to how long the sound hangs.

3. Book the musicians early

December is the busiest month of the musical year, so the earlier you start booking your choir, the wider your choice of date and ensemble. September is ideal for a December service. If your date is already close, ask anyway: we take last-minute Christmas bookings when we have singers free.

The choir does two jobs. It leads the room’s singing, holding the tempo and filling in the harmony so that hesitant singers have something to lean on, and it sings two or three pieces on its own: an unaccompanied anthem, or a descant on the last verse of a carol. Four singers will lead a room of about 150; eight will carry a church of 400.

Accompaniment. Most carol services use an organ, a piano or both. In a church with a good organ, use it: a full organ on the last verse of O Come, All Ye Faithful is hard to beat. In an office or hotel, a piano or a good digital keyboard does the same job. We can supply an organist or pianist at £250 alongside the choir.

4. Choose the carols and readings

Six to eight carols is the right number. With fewer the service feels slight, and after eight people start to tire, especially if they’re standing. Mix carols everyone can sing (Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, O Come, All Ye Faithful, Once in Royal David’s City) with one or two quieter ones such as Silent Night or In the Bleak Midwinter, and something less expected like the Sussex Carol. Our guide to Christmas carols covers the most popular choices in detail.

For readings, the model is the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols, first sung at King’s College, Cambridge, on Christmas Eve 1918: readings and carols alternate, building from quiet to grand. A workplace service doesn’t need nine. Three or four fit a 45-minute lunchtime service; an hour-long evening service can take five or six. Use one or two biblical passages if you want them (the Nativity from Luke’s Gospel is the obvious one) alongside poetry and prose: Dickens’s A Christmas Carol, Dylan Thomas’s A Child’s Christmas in Wales, Laurie Lee, U. A. Fanthorpe or John Betjeman’s poem Christmas. A short piece written in-house, a look back at the year, works if it is well written and under three minutes.

Choosing readers. Ask colleagues from different departments and levels of seniority. It is a mark of recognition and gives the service a personal quality no hired compere can supply. Brief them beforehand: stand up, speak slowly, project, keep to the text, and stop by three minutes.

5. Make a programme

People can’t sing what they can’t see. A single A5 card folded once is enough: the running order, the full words of every carol the room sings, and the readers’ names. Keep the design plain — your logo, the date, a clear typeface in a readable size — and hand one to each guest at the door.

6. Send the invitations

Give three to four weeks’ notice, and send client invitations earlier, in November. Keep the tone informal. Say that everyone is welcome whatever their faith or musical ability, that attendance is optional, and how long it will last. People come more readily when they know it won’t take the whole afternoon.

What the service looks like

A typical corporate carol service has:

  • A welcome from a senior leader, no longer than two minutes
  • Six to eight carols for the whole room, led by the choir
  • Two or three pieces sung by the choir alone, placed between the congregational carols
  • Three to six short readings, delivered by colleagues from across the organisation
  • A closing carol and a brief farewell from the host, then drinks if the venue allows

A lunchtime service runs about 45 minutes; an evening service for staff or clients usually runs an hour, with drinks and canapés afterwards to give people a reason to linger and talk. In a church with room to move around, some companies split the service in two with refreshments in the middle, which takes it to about ninety minutes.

Running order. Open with something everyone knows, such as Once in Royal David’s City or O Come, All Ye Faithful. Put a choir-only piece early, while attention is fresh. Alternate carols and readings, keep the quieter moments in the middle, and close with Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, the last hymn at King’s. Leave no more than a few seconds between items.

A sample running order

This is a 45-minute lunchtime service. For an hour-long evening service, add a congregational carol and one or two readings.

  1. Welcome (2 minutes) — a senior colleague or the office host says a few informal words.
  2. O Come, All Ye Faithful (congregational) — an opening carol everyone knows. The choir leads and the room follows.
  3. First reading: Charles Dickens (3 minutes) — the opening of A Christmas Carol, or the Fezziwig party from Stave Two, read by a colleague.
  4. In the Bleak Midwinter (choir) — Christina Rossetti’s poem in Holst’s setting, sung by the choir alone.
  5. Second reading: Dylan Thomas (3 minutes) — an extract from A Child’s Christmas in Wales.
  6. Hark! The Herald Angels Sing (congregational) — the choir adds a descant on the last verse.
  7. Third reading: a colleague (2 minutes) — a short reflection from a business leader or team member: thanks for the year, or a look ahead.
  8. Sussex Carol (choir) — a quick, rhythmic carol to lift the energy before the close.
  9. Silent Night (congregational) — quiet, ideally by candlelight.
  10. Closing words (1 minute) — the host thanks everyone and points them towards the mince pies.
  11. Drinks and mince pies — mulled wine, soft drinks and something to eat.

You might swap a reading for a poem, or start with a short choir piece while people settle. If you want the full liturgical shape, our guide to planning a Nine Lessons and Carols service lists the traditional readings.

Keeping it inclusive

Some colleagues will ask whether a carol service is too religious for a mixed workplace. You decide how religious it is. Say on the invitation that everyone is welcome regardless of belief and that attendance is optional, and you can call the event a winter celebration or an end-of-year music event if you prefer; the format stays the same.

Choose the carols with that in mind. Jingle Bells, written by James Lord Pierpont and published in 1857, and Winter Wonderland (1934) are secular, and O Christmas Tree is about the evergreen. Most of the traditional carols do tell the Christmas story: In the Bleak Midwinter, Christina Rossetti’s poem of 1872, is about the Nativity, though many people sing it, like Silent Night, as part of a British December rather than as a statement of faith.

Do the same with the readings. Dickens and Dylan Thomas are secular and well loved. Betjeman’s Christmas moves from shop windows and church bells to a closing statement of belief, so pair it with something lighter if you are aiming for balance. A colleague’s own reflection on the year sits comfortably in any version of the service.

What it costs

The main costs are the musicians, the venue, printed programmes and refreshments.

Musicians. In Greater London, a choir of four costs from £1,150, or £1,400 with an organist or pianist. A quintet costs £1,400, a sextet £1,600, a choir of eight £2,000 and a chorus of twelve £3,000. Each fee covers up to two hours on the day, including rehearsal; travel is added beyond Greater London. Our pricing page has the full list, and the Christmas choir hire guide explains which size suits which room.

Venue. Your own office costs nothing. Churches and hotels set their own hire fees, so ask the parish office or the events team for a quote when you check the date.

Programmes and refreshments. A run of A5 cards can be printed in-house or by a local printer. Mince pies and mulled wine are traditional and inexpensive, and many companies use their own catering team.

A lunchtime service in your own office with four singers and home-printed programmes can come in at little more than the choir’s fee. A client evening in a hired church with a choir of eight, an organist and a reception costs the musicians’ £2,250 plus whatever the church and the caterer charge. We’re happy to talk through what’s possible within your budget and give you a clear quote.

Planning timeline

  • September — decide on a date, start venue enquiries, and ask musicians for availability and a quote.
  • October — confirm the venue and book the choir. Start choosing carols and readings, and pick your readers.
  • November — finalise the running order, order the programmes, arrange catering, and send invitations to clients and colleagues.
  • Early December — brief your readers. Confirm access times, seating, candles and parking with the venue, and send the final running order to the musicians.
  • The day — the musicians arrive early to rehearse in the room. You welcome your guests.

Sound and amplification. In a church the choir won’t need amplification, though readers may want a lectern microphone in a large space. In an office or hotel, discuss it with the musicians beforehand; a small PA for the readers is often enough.

Accessibility. Check step-free access, because churches vary. Give clear directions, confirm parking or drop-off, and print the programme in a readable size. In your own building this is usually simpler, but still worth walking through.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving the musicians until last. Start in September and you’ll have the widest choice of date and ensemble.
  • Too many carols. People flag after eight, especially if they’re standing.
  • No printed words. This is the most common mistake. Without the words in front of them, people won’t sing, and the room singing is the point of the event.
  • An untested room. A low-ceilinged, carpeted open-plan office needs more singers than a hard-floored atrium. Describe the space to your musicians and they will tell you what it needs.
  • No coordinator on the day. Someone has to greet the musicians, cue the readers, lay out the programmes and keep to time. It doesn’t need to be a professional event manager, just someone who knows the plan and can make small decisions on the spot.

Frequently asked questions

How do you plan a corporate carol service?

Choose a date in the second or third week of December and a venue: a church, your own offices or a hotel function room. Book a professional choir by September, choose six to eight carols and three to six readings, ask colleagues to read, and print a programme with the words of every carol.

What do you need for an office carol service?

A room where everyone can stand together, a professional choir to lead the singing, a pianist or organist if you want accompaniment, printed programmes with the carol words, three or four readings with colleagues to read them, and someone to run the day. Mince pies and mulled wine afterwards are optional.

How long should an office carol service last?

About 45 minutes for a lunchtime service, which fits six to eight carols and three or four readings. An end-of-day service can run to an hour, followed by drinks and mince pies. A service split in two with refreshments in the middle runs to about ninety minutes.

How much does a corporate carol service cost?

In Greater London a choir of four costs from £1,150, a quintet £1,400, a sextet £1,600, a choir of eight £2,000 and a chorus of twelve £3,000, each for up to two hours including rehearsal. An organist or pianist adds £250. Venue hire and refreshments are extra.

Does a workplace carol service have to be religious?

No. You can mix traditional carols with secular songs such as Jingle Bells and Winter Wonderland, choose readings from Dickens or Dylan Thomas, and call the event a winter celebration. Say on the invitation that everyone is welcome regardless of belief and that attendance is optional.

How far in advance should I plan a corporate carol service?

Start in September for a December service. Churches and professional musicians are at their busiest in December, and the best dates go first. If your date is already close, ask anyway: last-minute bookings are possible when singers are free.
