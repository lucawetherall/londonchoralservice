Pop songs on the organ at your wedding

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 6 March 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

A good church organist can play most of the pop songs couples ask for, but some suit the instrument far better than others. A song with a strong tune and moving chords survives without its drums and electric guitar; a song built on a beat or on one singer’s voice does not. This guide covers the organ on its own: the pop, film and musical-theatre songs our organists play at weddings, where each fits in the service, and how to brief an organist on an arrangement. For pop songs sung by voices, see our guide to pop songs for a wedding choir; for the classical repertoire, see popular wedding organ music.

Can an organist play pop songs at a wedding? Yes. Most professional organists can arrange a pop song for the organ if you give them a few weeks. Songs with a strong tune and moving harmony work best: Can’t Help Falling in Love (Elvis Presley, 1961), Perfect (Ed Sheeran, 2017), A Thousand Years (Christina Perri, 2011) and Fix You (Coldplay, 2005), which already opens on an organ. Drum-led dance tracks rarely work.

We provide organists for weddings across London and the rest of the UK. An organist costs £250 for up to two hours, rehearsal included, in Greater London; a soloist and organist together cost £500.

Upbeat songs for the recessional

You want energy as you walk back down the aisle, and pop carries that better than most of the classical repertoire. The strongest upbeat songs on a church organ are the soul and gospel-inflected hits of the late sixties and seventies, whose chords come out of church music in the first place.

All You Need Is Love (The Beatles, 1967)

John Lennon wrote it (credited to Lennon–McCartney) for the Our World satellite broadcast in June 1967, and the Beatles opened the record with the first bars of “La Marseillaise”, a ready-made fanfare for the walk out. On full organ the chorus is joyful, and the organist can repeat the closing refrain until the last guests have left the church.

Ain’t No Mountain High Enough (Marvin Gaye & Tammi Terrell, 1967 / Diana Ross, 1970)

Nickolas Ashford and Valerie Simpson wrote it over a gospel-style chord progression, which an organist can play much as written. Marvin Gaye and Tammi Terrell sang the original duet; Diana Ross’s solo recording takes longer to build, which helps if the recessional needs to last.

Lovely Day (Bill Withers, 1977)

Bill Withers wrote it with Skip Scarborough. The bassline moves to the pedals, and the high note Withers holds for about eighteen seconds near the end is a small joke worth playing if your organist is up for it. The tempo is relaxed enough that nobody misses the drums, and few guests will expect it as you walk out.

Modern ballads

Couples ask us for modern ballads more than any other kind of pop song, and they move to the organ well. Many of the big wedding songs of the last twenty years are piano ballads, which gives the organist a keyboard part to start from.

Perfect (Ed Sheeran, 2017)

The modern wedding song we are asked for most. Sheeran wrote it in 12/8, a lilting compound time that a great deal of organ music shares, so it sits better on the instrument than a pop hit built on a 4/4 drum beat. Keep the first verse on flutes, add the swell for the chorus, and pull full great for the final repeat. The single runs 4 minutes 23 seconds, which covers the signing of the register and is a little long for the recessional.

A Thousand Years (Christina Perri, 2011)

Christina Perri and David Hodges wrote it for The Twilight Saga: Breaking Dawn – Part 1, and it is now a wedding standard in its own right. The piano arpeggios in the original map onto the organ’s manuals without much adjustment, and the soaring chorus sits well on a principal stop. Strongest during the signing of the register.

All of Me (John Legend, 2013)

John Legend wrote it with Toby Gad, and it is a piano song from start to finish, so the opening figure moves to the manuals more or less untouched. Legend directed a church choir as a teenager, and the suspensions and voice-leading give the organist plenty to work with. A strong choice for the signing of the register.

Make You Feel My Love (Bob Dylan, 1997 / Adele, 2008)

Dylan wrote it. Adele made it a wedding song. The melody behaves like a hymn, which is why it sounds at home in a church. Ask your organist to keep the registration soft through the verses and open out the full organ for the final chorus. It suits the signing of the register, or a quiet processional.

Fix You (Coldplay, 2005)

All four members of Coldplay share the writing credit, and their recording opens on an organ, so the song needs little translation. Take the first verse on a soft stop, add the second manual where the drums would have entered, and pull full great for the final chorus. The build is slow, so the song needs space; best for a substantial signing of the register, or a long procession of bridesmaids.

Marry Me (Train, 2010)

Pat Monahan wrote it for Train’s album Save Me, San Francisco (2009), and the single followed in 2010. The title makes it an obvious wedding choice. It is a slow ballad, and the chorus opens wider than the verses, so the organist can add stops as the song goes on. It fits the signing of the register.

Classic love songs

The mid-century love songs were written when songwriting leaned on melody rather than studio production, which is why they sit on the organ without much fuss. Younger and older guests both know them, and their chords often sit close to a hymn’s.

Can’t Help Falling in Love (Elvis Presley, 1961)

The single most-requested pop song at our weddings. Its writers, Hugo Peretti, Luigi Creatore and George David Weiss, took the melody from Martini’s “Plaisir d’amour” of 1784, and that classical bone structure is why it translates to the organ as cleanly as it does. A good organist will open on soft flute stops and build through the final verse to something close to a hymn. It lasts about three minutes, which suits a short processional or the close of the register signing.

At Last (Etta James, 1960)

Mack Gordon and Harry Warren wrote the song in 1941 for the film Orchestra Wives. Etta James’s 1960 recording made it a first-dance and ceremony staple. The long held notes of the melody suit the organ, and it gives the signing of the register a touch of glamour.

La Vie en Rose (Édith Piaf, 1947)

Piaf wrote the words in 1945 to a melody by Louiguy, and her recording came out in 1947. The tune has a cabaret chanson’s rich harmony and a folk song’s directness, and both survive on the organ. Piaf’s recording lasts about three minutes, and the organist can shorten it for a tight moment in the service.

Film and TV themes

Film themes are written for orchestra, which means they sit halfway between classical scoring and pop. That makes them some of the strongest candidates for an organ arrangement, because the harmonic writing is often already close to what an organ wants to do.

Main Title from Game of Thrones (Ramin Djawadi, 2011)

The cello ostinato translates to the organ’s pedals, the strings move to the manuals, and in a stone church the result has more power than couples expect. It suits a processional for a couple who want something dramatic.

Hedwig’s Theme from Harry Potter (John Williams, 2001)

Written in a minor key and built on a celesta figure in the original, the theme translates to flute stops on a small organ. It has an air of wonder that suits the prelude more than the processional. Best at a wedding where both of you grew up on the books.

Over the Rainbow from The Wizard of Oz (Harold Arlen, 1939)

Harold Arlen wrote the music and Yip Harburg the words, and almost everyone knows the octave leap on “Somewhere”. The organ holds the long opening note and the stepwise descent without strain, and the chorus builds to a ceiling-filling sound if you want it. Ideal for the register signing, or as a soft recessional for a summer wedding.

Cinema Paradiso love theme (Andrea Morricone, 1988)

The score is Ennio Morricone’s, but the love theme is by his son Andrea. It is tender, slow-moving, and full of space, and it works during the signing of the register when you want a moment of stillness before the recessional.

The Princess Bride: Storybook Love (Mark Knopfler, 1987)

Less famous than the others, and romantic. Knopfler’s melody has a Renaissance-folk quality that suits a country church. If you want a piece few of your guests will have heard before, this is a strong candidate.

Musical theatre

Show tunes often work well on the organ because they were written for a big stage sound in the first place. The organ gives you the orchestra pit without needing one.

All I Ask of You from The Phantom of the Opera (Andrew Lloyd Webber, 1986)

Charles Hart wrote the words to Lloyd Webber’s duet. The melody lies well under the fingers, the harmonic movement is straightforward, and the final key change is built to lift a room. Suits the signing of the register, where you have the time to let the full arc of the song play out.

Seasons of Love from Rent (Jonathan Larson, 1996)

The gospel-inflected chord progression is a perfect organ sound. The song is upbeat without being fussy, and it works as a recessional or as the final piece of a prelude set. Couples who first met at university tend to love it.

Can You Feel the Love Tonight from The Lion King (Elton John & Tim Rice, 1994)

Elton John writes at the piano, and his chord voicings move to the organ almost untouched. It is a gentle choice, strongest during the register signing, and it lands especially well with couples who grew up on Disney in the nineties.

Memory from Cats (Andrew Lloyd Webber, 1981)

Trevor Nunn wrote the words. The slow build of the melody is what an organ does well. Choose this for the prelude as guests arrive, not the processional; the pace is too reflective for walking down the aisle. A soloist can also sing it with the organ if you want a voice on the day; for pop songs sung by voices alone, see our guide to pop songs for a wedding choir.

What works, and what doesn’t

Not every pop song translates. Before you commit to a title, ask three questions of it.

Does the melody carry without the vocals? Songs that rely on the singer’s phrasing, breathiness, or lyrical delivery often lose their identity on the organ. Whitney Houston’s I Will Always Love You is a good example: the drama is in the voice, and the organ cannot reproduce it. By contrast, Elvis’s Can’t Help Falling in Love has a melody so strong it survives any instrumentation.

Is it harmonically interesting? Pop songs built on three or four chords can feel thin when you take away the production. Songs with moving bass lines, surprising chord changes, or clear voice-leading (Elton John, Billy Joel, Beatles) suit the organ because they give the organist something to work with.

Does it depend on drums? The organ has no percussion. Songs that live and die on their beat (most modern R&B, dance pop, anything by Bruno Mars that isn’t a ballad) sound strange without it. If the song is defined by its rhythm, pick something else.

If you’re unsure, ask your organist to play you thirty seconds of the chorus before you commit. Any good organist will be happy to do this.

Where in the ceremony to place a pop song

The prelude (the fifteen minutes as guests arrive) is the safest slot for anything non-traditional. Guests are chatting, nobody is watching the altar, and a pop organ arrangement becomes a pleasant background surprise rather than the main event.

The signing of the register is the second-best slot. The ceremony pauses, the organist gets five to eight minutes of uninterrupted focus, and you can choose something personal without disrupting the flow of the service.

The recessional carries pop songs well if they have enough energy. All You Need Is Love, Signed, Sealed, Delivered, or anything bright and fast suits the walk back down the aisle.

The processional is the hardest slot for a pop song. The walking pace is slower than most songs are written at, and the emotional weight of the moment often calls for something grander than a pop ballad delivers. If you’re set on a pop processional, pick a song with a confident, steady tempo (Can’t Help Falling in Love at a deliberate pace, or the Game of Thrones theme), and talk the arrangement through with your organist ahead of time. Working with your organist on an arrangement

Most professional organists can arrange a pop song, given time. Eight weeks is comfortable. Four weeks is the bare minimum. Less than that and the organist will either sight-read something from the internet (quality varies) or arrange something under pressure.

Send a recording of the exact version you want. “A Thousand Years” has a piano version, an orchestral version, and a version re-recorded for Breaking Dawn – Part 2; the organist needs to know which one is in your head. A YouTube link is enough.

Tell them where in the ceremony the piece sits, and how long you need it to last. A processional that runs three minutes needs a different arrangement from one that runs ninety seconds. Good organists will build in flexibility, so they can shorten or lengthen on the day based on how the walk is going.

If your organist is a friend of the family, or a church organist who doesn’t play pop music often, ask the question outright. Some are wonderful Bach players who will struggle with this. If the church agrees, we can supply an organist who plays pop arrangements, alongside or instead of the resident player.

Frequently asked questions

Can an organist play modern songs at a wedding?

Yes. Most professional church organists play arrangements of pop songs, film themes and musical-theatre numbers. The organ suits songs with a smooth melody and rich harmony, such as Can’t Help Falling in Love, Perfect, A Thousand Years and All of Me. Tracks built on drums or dense studio production work less well.

Is it appropriate to play pop songs on the organ at a church wedding?

It depends on the church. In the Church of England the parish minister has the final say on the music at a wedding (Canons B 20 and B 35), and many allow a pop song before the service, during the signing of the register or as you leave. Catholic parishes are often stricter about secular music. Send your music list to the minister when you book the church.

How far ahead should I ask my organist for a pop song arrangement?

Give your organist eight weeks for a bespoke arrangement; four weeks is the minimum. If you book an organist through The London Choral Service, we can arrange the song for you. Depending on the piece, an arrangement can carry a small extra charge, agreed upfront.

Do we have to use the church’s own organist?

We can supply an organist who plays pop arrangements, for £250 in Greater London. If your church has its own organist, tell us when you enquire and we will speak to the church about who plays.

Can a singer perform a pop song with the organ?

Yes. A soloist with organ is the simplest way to have the words sung as well as the tune played, and it suits slower songs such as Make You Feel My Love or Memory. A soloist and organist together cost £500 in Greater London.
