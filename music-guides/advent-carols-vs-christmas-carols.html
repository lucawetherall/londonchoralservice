Advent carols vs Christmas carols

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 31 July 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

Advent and Christmas are two seasons with two repertoires, and many December carol services mix them without meaning to. At an office or hotel event nobody will complain. In a church the incumbent may have views, so learn the distinction before you build the programme.

What is the difference between Advent carols and Christmas carols? Advent carols belong to the four weeks before Christmas and look forward to a coming that has not happened yet, usually in restrained, modal music; O Come, O Come, Emmanuel, built on the O Antiphons sung from 17 to 23 December, is the best known. Christmas carols, such as O Come, All Ye Faithful, celebrate the birth as done, and belong from 25 December to Epiphany.

Two seasons, not one

Advent runs from the fourth Sunday before Christmas to Christmas Eve. Christmas begins on 25 December and runs twelve days to Epiphany. Almost every carol service held in the UK happens during Advent, which is the source of the confusion: people gather in Advent to sing Christmas carols.

Advent is a season of waiting and preparation. Its texts look forward — to a coming, to a promise not yet kept — and its music is correspondingly restrained, often modal and often in a minor key. Christmas texts announce that the thing has happened.

Liturgically the two are as distinct as Lent and Easter. In practice most December services blur them, and the pragmatic question is not whether to blur but how much.

The Advent repertoire

O Come, O Come, Emmanuel

The Advent carol. The text is built from the O Antiphons, sung on the seven days before Christmas Eve, and the tune is a fifteenth-century French processional. Mary Berry found the melody in 1966 in a fifteenth-century French manuscript of processional chants, and John Mason Neale published the English words in 1851. It is modal and unhurried, and the refrain, “Rejoice! Emmanuel shall come to thee, O Israel”, is in the future tense.

Come, Thou Long Expected Jesus

Charles Wesley wrote it in 1744, and the title says the whole thing. It is plain Advent, and churches sing it as a hymn more often than as a carol.

Lo, He Comes with Clouds Descending

Advent in its other mode: Charles Wesley’s hymn of 1758, reworking John Cennick’s of 1752, is about the second coming and the last judgement, and it is more alarming than most people expect from a December hymn. Sung to Helmsley it is one of the grandest things in the English repertoire.

On Jordan’s Bank and Hills of the North, Rejoice

Both belong to the preparation theme: John the Baptist, and the summons to make ready. Neither mentions a stable.

Adam Lay Ybounden

The text is fifteenth-century, and it suits Advent because it is about the fall that, in the carol’s own logic, made the incarnation necessary. Boris Ord’s setting, sung at King’s College, Cambridge, is the one most choirs know.

The Christmas repertoire

Everything that describes the birth as having happened: O Come, All Ye Faithful, Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, Silent Night, Away in a Manger, In the Bleak Midwinter, O Little Town of Bethlehem.

Once in Royal David’s City is Christmas, though its use as the opening of Nine Lessons and Carols on Christmas Eve puts it right on the boundary.

Epiphany carols are a third category, and they are often sung too early. We Three Kings, written by John Henry Hopkins in 1857, belongs to 6 January, not to a service on 12 December: the church keeps the Wise Men’s visit at Epiphany, after the shepherds of Christmas night. For the Christmas carols that work best at a service and how to order them, see our guide to the best carols for a carol service.

Does it matter?

For a corporate carol service, a hotel lobby or a residents’ evening: no. Nobody is keeping liturgical time at a drinks reception, and a programme of Advent restraint would disappoint a room that came to hear Ding Dong! Merrily on High.

For a service in a church, it depends entirely on the incumbent. Some are relaxed; some hold the line firmly and will not have Christmas carols before the fourth Sunday of Advent. Ask early, because it is a decision that shapes the whole programme and it is not yours to make.

The middle position, which most parishes take, is an Advent carol service in early December that keeps some Advent material and admits the best-known Christmas carols at the end. That is a defensible shape and it satisfies almost everybody.

Building a service that sits in Advent

An Advent carol service is a real form with its own shape: it moves from darkness towards light, both musically and often literally, with the church lit progressively as the service goes on. The readings run from the prophets through to the annunciation rather than to the nativity.

A workable programme opens with O Come, O Come, Emmanuel, keeps the middle in Advent territory with Adam Lay Ybounden, an O Antiphon setting and something from the Advent Prose, and finishes with one or two Christmas carols as the light comes up. Ending in Advent is possible and takes some nerve; a congregation that has waited an hour usually wants the arrival.

Our guide to carols by candlelight covers the lighting side, which matters more for an Advent service than for a Christmas one.

The O Antiphons

Seven ancient texts sung at Vespers on 17 to 23 December, each addressing Christ by a different title: Wisdom, Lord, Root of Jesse, Key of David, Dayspring, King of Nations, Emmanuel. They are the source of O Come, O Come, Emmanuel, whose verses are the antiphons in metrical translation.

The initials of the Latin titles (Sapientia, Adonai, Radix, Clavis, Oriens, Rex, Emmanuel), read backwards, spell ero cras, “tomorrow I shall be [there]”. Scholars disagree over whether that was designed or noticed later. Either way it makes good lectern material.

Choirs sometimes sing one antiphon as a choir-only item in an Advent service, which is the most direct way to put genuine Advent material into a programme that is otherwise heading for Christmas.

Advent in a secular programme

Even where liturgy is irrelevant, the Advent repertoire is worth raiding for one practical reason: contrast. A programme of nine cheerful major-key Christmas carols flattens out. One modal Advent piece in the middle resets the room’s ear and makes the carol after it sound brighter than it would have on its own.

O Come, O Come, Emmanuel is the piece that does this best and needs no explanation to a secular audience, because most people know it without knowing what season it belongs to.

The same trick works with the darker traditional carols. The Coventry Carol is technically Christmas, being about the massacre of the innocents, but it does the same job of changing the temperature.

Where the boundary actually falls in practice

The fourth Sunday of Advent is the point most parishes relax. Before it, Advent material and restraint; after it, Christmas carols are fair game because Christmas Eve is days away. A service on 20 December is treated very differently from one on 5 December, even in the same building.

Schools are the exception at the other end. A school carol service usually falls in the second week of December because that is when term ends, and most clergy accept it.

Cathedral and collegiate foundations tend to hold the line hardest, because they sing the full Advent liturgy anyway and a carol service is one item in a season rather than the whole of it.

Telling the audience which is which

If your programme mixes the two, one sentence in the order of service does a lot of work: a note that the first half looks forward and the second half arrives. It turns what could read as muddle into a deliberate shape.

This also gives whoever is introducing the carols something worth saying. “This one is about waiting; the next one is about arriving” is a better line from the lectern than a summary of the plot.

For a corporate audience it is worth about thirty seconds and no more. The distinction is interesting and it is not what anybody came for.

Common mistakes

Singing We Three Kings in Advent. Epiphany material at a December service is the most frequent slip and the one a clergy reader is most likely to notice.

Treating O Come, O Come, Emmanuel as a Christmas carol. It is the single most Advent thing in the repertoire, and putting it after the nativity reading reverses the story.

Assuming the incumbent will not mind. Ask. It costs one email and it avoids a conversation in the vestry twenty minutes before the service.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an Advent carol and a Christmas carol?

Advent carols look forward to a coming that has not yet happened and tend to be modal and restrained. Christmas carols announce the birth as an event that has occurred. O Come, O Come, Emmanuel is Advent; O Come, All Ye Faithful is Christmas.

Can you sing Christmas carols during Advent?

At a corporate event, hotel or residents' evening, yes, and nobody will mind. In a church it depends on the incumbent: some are relaxed, others will not have Christmas carols before the fourth Sunday of Advent. Ask before you build the programme.

When should We Three Kings be sung?

At Epiphany, 6 January, rather than at a December carol service. The church keeps the visit of the Wise Men at Epiphany, after the shepherds of Christmas night, and singing it in Advent is the slip a clergy reader is most likely to notice.

What are the O Antiphons?

Seven ancient texts sung at Vespers from 17 to 23 December, each addressing Christ by a different title. They are the source of the verses of O Come, O Come, Emmanuel, and the initials of the Latin titles read backwards spell ero cras, tomorrow I shall be.

What is the difference between a carol and a hymn?

A hymn is a song of praise written for a congregation to sing in worship, at any season. A carol, from the Old French carole, a circle dance, began as a festive song with verses and a refrain, often religious but sung outside church too. Many Christmas carols are hymns by origin: Charles Wesley wrote Hark! The Herald Angels Sing in 1739 as a hymn for Christmas Day.

When is Advent?

From the fourth Sunday before Christmas Day to Christmas Eve, so it starts between 27 November and 3 December. In 2026 Advent Sunday falls on 29 November. Most carol services happen in Advent, which is why the two repertoires get mixed.

We sing both Advent and Christmas services, and Luca will build a programme that fits whichever season your date actually falls in, including squaring it with the incumbent where that matters.
