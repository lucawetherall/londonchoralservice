Hiring a UK choir for a destination wedding

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 29 August 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

Couples who have heard a choir sing in an English church often want that sound at a wedding in Tuscany, the Algarve or Barbados, and assume it is impossible or absurdly expensive. It is a different kind of booking from hiring singers at home. This guide covers what sets the cost, how far ahead you need to commit, the work-permit question few couples raise until it is late, and what changes musically when there is no organ and no stone.

Can you hire a UK choir for a wedding abroad? Yes. Alma Consort, our performing ensemble, takes eight to twenty-four singers from London to weddings in Europe, the Caribbean, the Americas, the Indian Ocean and beyond. The singers rehearse in London, fly out together and rehearse in the building the day before. You get one quote covering the singing, flights and rooms, and the United States needs months of extra notice for visas.

At home, our wedding rates are published: a choir of eight costs £2,000 and a chorus of twelve £3,000 in Greater London. A wedding abroad is quoted whole before you commit.

What you are paying for

Three things move the figure, and only one of them is the music: the number of singers, the distance they travel, and the nights they stay.

Our UK rates are published on the pricing page: £2,000 for a choir of eight and £3,000 for a chorus of twelve in Greater London. The consort that travels abroad starts at eight voices. A wedding abroad adds return flights and accommodation for every singer, and a night before the wedding, because a consort that has not sung in the building is guessing at it. We quote the whole engagement before you commit, and invoice in pounds sterling, euros or US dollars.

For a wedding in Italy or Portugal, travel is a real but manageable addition to the music budget. For the Maldives or Bali, the flights alone will exceed what many weddings set aside for music altogether. You can still do it, but start the conversation with the number rather than end with it.

One thing that does not change is the size of the group you need. A church that wanted eight voices in Hampshire wants eight voices in Puglia. Nobody saves money by shipping a quartet to do a choir’s work.

The weekend, not the ceremony

Most couples work this out too late to act on it. A destination wedding lasts three or four days, with everyone you care about in one place, having travelled to get there. The ceremony might use twenty minutes of it.

And the expensive part of bringing a choir out  —  the fares and the rooms  —  is spent whether we sing once or four times. Singing at the welcome dinner on the Friday, at the ceremony on the Saturday, over drinks afterwards, and unaccompanied in a stone room after the meal costs far less in total than those four appearances would suggest, because only the first one carries the travel.

It also solves a problem couples do not always anticipate. The evenings either side of the wedding day are usually the parts nobody has planned, and they are the ones where guests who have flown a long way are sitting around wondering what happens next. Something worth listening to, in a room with stone in it, is a good answer.

Lead times, and the one that catches people out

Six to twelve months is the usual window for Europe, and in practice you want the longer end. Clearing twelve professional diaries for a Saturday is the easy half; booking flights and rooms for twelve people on a summer weekend in Amalfi is the half that fails if you leave it.

The United States works differently from everywhere else. Performing for a fee there is not allowed on a tourist visa or the visa waiver programme: each musician needs a performance visa (an O or P category), petitioned for in advance on USCIS Form I-129, and the timeline is measured in months. A couple who decide in March on a September wedding in New York have a music problem they do not yet know about. If you are considering the States, raise it at the first conversation, and treat the visa calendar rather than the venue calendar as the binding constraint.

Elsewhere the paperwork varies. Inside the Schengen area British visitors may stay ninety days in any one hundred and eighty, and each singer carries a certificate of coverage from HMRC (often called an A1) showing that they stay in UK National Insurance while they work there. Whether a short paid performance also needs a permit depends on the member state, and we check it for each booking. Several long-haul destinations have their own requirements for paid performers, and all of it is harder to sort out late. Our guide on how to book a choir covers the rest of the booking, from the first enquiry to the deposit.

Which parts get sung, and in what language

The biggest musical variable abroad is which kind of ceremony you choose, and that is largely up to you. A humanist, non-denominational or celebrant-led ceremony has no liturgy at all, so the music sits wherever you want it: the entrance, the signing, the exit, and whatever you ask for around the vows. That is the most common choice among couples marrying abroad, and the most flexible. An Anglican service will feel like a wedding at home, and is available in far more countries than couples realise  —  the Diocese in Europe has English-speaking chaplaincies across the Continent, and Commonwealth countries have Anglican churches of their own. A Catholic nuptial Mass has a sung ordinary, a psalm and acclamations, and gives a choir more to do than anything else. Local rites  —  Greek Orthodox, Balinese Hindu, a Thai Buddhist blessing  —  come into it mainly where one partner is local or where you want a blessing alongside your own ceremony; they have their own musicians and sit beside what we do rather than instead of it.

Language follows from the rite. We sing the Latin ordinary as standard, and in Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese and German where the ceremony calls for it. Where the congregation is split between two languages, the usual solution is to sing the ordinary in Latin, which belongs to neither family and is recognisable to both, and to keep the congregational hymns in whichever language most of the guests will sing.

For the repertoire itself, our guides to choral pieces for a wedding and choosing wedding hymns apply the same abroad as at home; what changes is where they sit in the service.

No organ, no stage, no stone

Most destination weddings happen somewhere that was never built for music: a terrace, a cloister, a lawn, a beach. This suits an unaccompanied consort better than it suits a string quartet or a band, because there is nothing to plug in, nothing to tune, and nothing to strike afterwards.

What it does change is the number of voices, and there are two separate questions inside that. The first is the room. A stone church returns sound and flatters a small group; an outdoor ceremony returns nothing at all, and a carpeted ballroom actively absorbs it.

The second is whether anyone is listening. During a ceremony the room is quiet and the building decides the number. During drinks or dinner it is not, and unamplified voices have to carry over the conversation  —  which does scale with how many people are having it. This is the one case where the guest list moves the answer, and it is why a reception set often wants more voices than the ceremony did in the same venue.

For the ceremony itself, the rule of thumb is that a space with no reverberation wants more singers than the same ceremony would need indoors  —  twelve where a chapel would have been happy with eight. Heat matters too: a group singing at four in the afternoon in the Caribbean needs shade and water in a way it does not in Winchester, and that belongs in the running order rather than being discovered on the day.

Questions couples ask

Can you hire a UK choir for a wedding abroad?

Yes. Alma Consort, our performing ensemble, takes eight to twenty-four singers from London to weddings abroad. They rehearse in London, fly out together, arrive the day before and rehearse in the building. The complications are administrative: chiefly whether the country requires a permit before a British musician may be paid to perform, and how far ahead that has to be arranged.

How much does it cost to fly a choir to your wedding?

Three things move the number: how many singers, how far they travel, and how many nights they stay. In Greater London a choir of eight costs £2,000 and a chorus of twelve £3,000. An engagement abroad adds return flights and rooms for every singer, plus the night before for rehearsal, so on long-haul dates the travel often costs more than the singing. We quote the whole engagement before you commit.

How far in advance should we book?

Six to twelve months for Europe, and nearer twelve than six in practice: booking flights and accommodation for twelve to twenty-four people takes longer than clearing their diaries. The United States needs far longer, because paid performance there runs through a visa petition with a lead time measured in months rather than weeks.

Do British musicians need a visa or work permit to perform abroad?

It depends on the country, and few couples think to ask. Inside the Schengen area the singers work within the ninety-days-in-any-one-hundred-and-eighty limit and carry social-security certificates, and some member states also want a permit for paid performance. The United States requires a petition-based performance visa. Several long-haul destinations have their own rules for paid performers. Ask before you fix a date, not after.

What if there is no church, no organ and no stage?

That describes most destination weddings, and it suits a consort better than it suits almost any other kind of live music. Unaccompanied singing needs no instrument, no power and no amplification. What changes is the number of voices: an open terrace or a beach gives nothing back acoustically, so it wants more singers than a stone chapel would.

Can the choir sing at the welcome dinner and the reception as well?

Yes, and abroad it is usually the sensible plan. The fares and the rooms are spent whether the consort sings once or four times, so a welcome dinner, the ceremony, the drinks afterwards and a late unaccompanied set cost far less together than they look. A reception set often wants more voices than the ceremony, because the singers have to carry over a talking room.

Do you invoice in euros or US dollars?

Yes. We invoice weddings abroad in pounds sterling, euros or US dollars, to the couple, their planner or the venue. Deposit and balance terms are set out in the booking agreement before anything is confirmed, and no VAT is added.
