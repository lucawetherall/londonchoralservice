Funeral hymns: the most popular choices in the UK

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 28 February 2026 · Updated 25 October 2026

Most funerals have two or three hymns, and they are the moments when everyone in the room does the same thing: stands, opens the order of service and sings a tune they already know. This guide covers the hymns UK families choose most, where each one sits in a service, and how to settle on two or three that suit the person.

What are the most popular funeral hymns? The hymns UK families ask us for most are The Lord’s My Shepherd, sung to Crimond, for its Psalm 23 words; Abide with Me, Henry Francis Lyte’s hymn of 1847; The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended, a gentle last hymn; Dear Lord and Father of Mankind, a prayer for stillness; and Guide Me, O Thou Great Redeemer, for full voice.

We provide funeral singers and organists to lead the hymns at funerals across London and the rest of the UK.

These eleven hymns come up most often. The table gives the tune, who wrote the words and where each usually sits; the notes below explain why families pick them. We have filmed Abide with Me, Be Thou My Vision and There’s a Wideness in God’s Mercy, so you can hear how a small choir leads them.

No single hymn suits every funeral. The ones below have stayed in use for generations because a congregation can sing them, the words are clear, and they carry feeling without sentimentality.

Recorded songs now take a larger share of the music. In Co-op Funeralcare’s 2025 chart, drawn from a firm that conducts more than 90,000 funerals a year, Time to Say Goodbye came first and Abide with Me, at number eight, was the only hymn in the top ten; in its 2019 poll no hymn made the top ten at all. Hymns are still the music the whole congregation sings together, and most church funerals we sing at include two or three of them.

The hymns families choose most

The Lord’s My Shepherd (Crimond)

The words are a metrical version of Psalm 23 from the Scottish Psalter of 1650. The tune Crimond, first published in 1872, is usually credited to Jessie Seymour Irvine, though some hymn books still name David Grant, who harmonised it. Many people who rarely go to church can sing it from memory, and the melody moves slowly enough for a congregation to keep singing through tears. It fits at any point in a service. It was sung at Queen Elizabeth II’s wedding in 1947 and again at her funeral in 2022. Our guide to The Lord’s My Shepherd prints all five verses.

Abide with Me

Henry Francis Lyte wrote the words in 1847, while he was dying of tuberculosis; he died that November. The tune most people know, Eventide, is by William Henry Monk (1861). The melody climbs without hurrying, and the words face death without pretending it is easy. Families often choose it as the closing hymn, when the tributes are over. Our guide to Abide with Me covers its history and how it sounds with a choir.

The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended

John Ellerton wrote this evening hymn in 1870. It is sung to St Clement, a tune first printed in 1874 and credited to Clement Scholefield. The words describe the sun setting on one congregation as it rises on another, so the hymn closes a funeral on a note of continuity. It was sung at Queen Elizabeth II’s funeral at Westminster Abbey on 19 September 2022.

How Great Thou Art

Carl Boberg wrote the Swedish poem O Store Gud in 1885, and it came to be sung to a Swedish folk tune; Stuart K. Hine’s English version dates from 1949. The tune swells into a broad refrain that lifts a full church. Families choose it when they want the congregation to sing out, and it needs a confident lead at the top of the refrain.

Amazing Grace

People who never go to church know the first verse, and that recognition gives them the confidence to sing. The words speak of rescue and hope in plain language. It suits religious and less formal services alike, and it sounds as good unaccompanied as it does with an organ.

Jerusalem

Hubert Parry’s setting of William Blake’s poem sounds more like an anthem than a hymn: bold, English, and loud enough to give a subdued congregation leave to sing out. Families choose it for people who lived with energy and purpose, often as the opening hymn.

Guide Me, O Thou Great Redeemer

William Williams wrote the Welsh original, and Peter Williams’s English translation appeared in 1771. The tune, Cwm Rhondda, is by John Hughes and dates from 1907. It drives forward, and the repeated last line of each verse lets a congregation build as it goes. It suits large churches and families who want the music strong.

Make Me a Channel of Your Peace

Sebastian Temple wrote this hymn on the prayer often attributed to St Francis of Assisi; the prayer first appeared in a French Catholic magazine in 1912, and it is absent from Francis’s own writings. The tune is simple and quiet. It works as a middle hymn between two louder ones, and families pick it for someone known for their kindness.

Dear Lord and Father of Mankind

The words come from John Greenleaf Whittier’s poem The Brewing of Soma (1872). The tune, Repton, is by Hubert Parry, taken from his oratorio Judith of 1888, and was first sung to these words at Repton School in 1924. The text asks for stillness — “the silence of eternity, interpreted by love” — and the calm melody draws a congregation in. It suits a reflective point in the service.

All Things Bright and Beautiful

Families choose it for gardeners and for people who loved the outdoors. The tune, Royal Oak, is bright and simple enough for children to join in, and it gives a service a tone of thanks.

Be Thou My Vision

An old Irish prayer in English translation, sung to the Irish folk tune Slane. The words ask for guidance and closeness to God and suit someone of steady faith. Fewer people know the tune than the others here, so it helps to have a few singers leading it. See our guide to Be Thou My Vision at funerals.

Hymns with personal meaning

Some families want a hymn the congregation may not know: a harvest hymn the person loved as a child, a carol they sang every Christmas, a hymn from their school chapel.

If a hymn matters to your family, put it in the service. Our musicians learn any hymn you choose and sing it with enough confidence for the room to follow. Mention it when you get in touch and we’ll find the music.

There’s a Wideness in God’s Mercy is one such hymn. Frederick William Faber wrote the words, and in our film twelve of our singers lead it to the tune Coverdale. Fewer congregations know it than Abide with Me, so a choir at the front gives them the tune to follow.

How many hymns should you have?

Most funeral services have two or three hymns. That fits the shape of a service without turning it into a concert.

With three, a common pattern is an opening hymn as the service begins, a hymn in the middle (often after a reading or the address), and a closing hymn before the commendation or as people leave. Choose a robust opening hymn that people can sing before emotion takes hold. The middle hymn can be gentler. The closing hymn comes after the tributes, when people feel the loss most, so families often give it the most weight.

Two hymns suit shorter services, especially at a crematorium with a fixed slot. Families in Bromley, Croydon and other outer London boroughs often use crematoriums with short slots, and one hymn near the start and one to close still gives the congregation two chances to sing together.

Your officiant or funeral director will place the hymns in the order of service, and we agree the timings and the music with them before the day.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most popular funeral hymns?

The hymns families choose most often include The Lord’s My Shepherd (Crimond), Abide with Me, The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended, How Great Thou Art, Amazing Grace, Jerusalem, Guide Me, O Thou Great Redeemer, Dear Lord and Father of Mankind, and All Things Bright and Beautiful.

What hymn is most commonly sung at funerals?

The Lord’s My Shepherd, sung to the tune Crimond, is the hymn we are asked for most. Its words are a metrical Psalm 23 from the 1650 Scottish Psalter, and many people who rarely go to church can sing it from memory. Abide with Me comes close, often as the closing hymn.

What is a good hymn to end a funeral?

Abide with Me and The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended are the usual last hymns. Both are evening hymns about the close of a day, and both have steady tunes a grieving congregation can manage. Families who want a stronger finish choose Guide Me, O Thou Great Redeemer.

What hymns were sung at Queen Elizabeth II’s funeral?

The state funeral at Westminster Abbey on 19 September 2022 included three hymns: The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended, The Lord’s My Shepherd, and Love Divine, All Loves Excelling. The Lord’s My Shepherd had also been sung at her wedding in 1947.

Are hymns still sung at UK funerals?

Yes, though recorded songs now take a larger share. In Co-op Funeralcare’s 2025 chart of the music chosen at its funerals, Time to Say Goodbye came first and Abide with Me was the only hymn in the top ten. Most church funerals we sing at still include two or three hymns.

Are there funeral hymns for non-churchgoers?

Yes. Amazing Grace and Jerusalem are familiar well beyond church. Professional singers can also learn any hymn a family chooses and lead it with enough confidence for the congregation to follow.
