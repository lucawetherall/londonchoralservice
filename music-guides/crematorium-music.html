Choosing music for a crematorium service

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 3 March 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

A crematorium service is shorter than a church funeral and runs to a fixed slot, with the next family arriving soon after yours leaves. This guide covers how the music fits that slot, how the chapel’s recorded music is played, what the room sounds like and where live singers help most.

What music can you have at a crematorium? Almost anything. Most services have music at three points: as the coffin comes in, during a moment of reflection or a hymn, and as people leave. Recordings play through the chapel’s system, often Obitus or Wesley Music, with tracks ordered by your funeral director. Live singers and organists are allowed too. A single slot is usually 30 to 40 minutes.

We send singers to funerals at crematoria across London and the rest of the UK: a soloist is £250, a Small Choir of four £1,150, and an organist £250.

How a crematorium service is structured

Each crematorium sets its own slot length. At Mortlake Crematorium a weekday slot is 40 minutes, about 30 of them for the service and the rest for coming in and leaving, and the two earliest slots are 30 minutes. The City of London Crematorium gives 30 minutes before 10am and 40 minutes from 10.15am. Both offer a double slot of 80 minutes at extra cost; Mortlake suggests one if you expect more than 75 people or several tributes. Your funeral director books the time, so ask them early if the programme looks long.

The usual shape runs like this. Entry music plays as the coffin is brought in and the congregation settles. An opening hymn follows, then a reading or two, a tribute from family or friends, perhaps a solo or choral piece, a second hymn, the committal, and exit music as people leave. Some families play a favourite recording by the original artist at one point and have live music at the others.

With less time than in a church, choose fewer pieces and give each one room. Our guide to how to choose music for a funeral covers where each piece sits in a service, and most of its advice applies here too.

The space and acoustics

Most crematorium chapels are smaller than a church, with lower ceilings and often carpet. Sound stays close instead of ringing round a stone roof, which changes what works best.

A solo voice carries well when the singer stands a few metres from the family. A choir of four fills the room without overpowering it. Our singers check the room before the service and set their volume to it.

Instruments vary from chapel to chapel. Mortlake keeps a pipe organ, played from the gallery out of sight; other chapels have a digital organ or a keyboard, and some rely on recorded music alone. Ask your funeral director what the chapel has. If there is no usable instrument, we can bring a portable keyboard, which may carry an extra charge, and we arrange it with the funeral director.

A soloist or small choir standing in the chapel sounds closer to the family than any recording through the speakers. We sing at crematoria across London and the rest of the UK.

Live music at a crematorium

Crematoria allow live musicians. Some families assume live music belongs only in a church, but a smaller room and a smaller congregation are where a singer makes the most difference.

A soloist or a choir of four suits the space. They lead the hymns, which matters when a small congregation feels unsure about singing, sing a piece during the reflection, and can sing as people come in and leave.

Our musicians arrive early, set up before the family and guests come in, and agree the running order with the funeral director: when to begin, when a recording plays, when to stop. You do not need to manage them on the day. Our services page explains how we work, and our guide to hiring a choir covers what to expect from professional singers at a funeral.

Recorded music

Many families want one particular recording: the original artist’s version of a favourite song, or a track that means something to the family. Crematoria play recordings through a chapel music system run by a specialist supplier. Mortlake and South Essex use Obitus, whose licensed library holds more than 300,000 tracks; other crematoria, such as Parc Gwyn in Pembrokeshire, use Wesley Music. Your funeral director orders the tracks and sets the order before the day. If a piece is not in the library, they can usually order it in advance, so give them your choices early.

You can mix the two. A singer might lead the hymns and sing a solo, with a recording of Frank Sinatra or Eva Cassidy at another point. The recording brings the person’s own music into the room, and the singers give the congregation a lead. Our guide to funeral songs covers popular recordings across many genres.

If you have both live and recorded music, we agree the running order with the funeral director beforehand, so the singers know which cue follows which track. You do not need to handle any of it on the day.

Choosing hymns for a crematorium

Two hymns fit a 30-minute service comfortably, where a church funeral often has three. Choose well-known hymns the congregation will feel confident singing.

“The Lord’s My Shepherd” (Crimond), “Abide with Me”, “Dear Lord and Father of Mankind” and “The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended” are common choices for that reason. People know the tunes, or half-remember them, and that gives a small congregation the courage to sing.

A crematorium congregation may be 30 or 40 people, many of them upset, and without a lead the singing can falter. One trained voice steadies it. A choir of four gives the room a full sound to sing with. Our guide to popular funeral hymns has notes on each of the hymns families choose most.

Making the most of a shorter service

Thirty to forty minutes is enough for a complete service. Families sometimes worry that a crematorium service will feel rushed, and a short programme planned in advance prevents that.

Give each piece one job. The entry music sets the tone while people sit down. A hymn brings the room together. A solo gives a minute of stillness after the tribute. The exit music plays as people walk out. One hymn and one solo, well sung, can move a room more than five pieces squeezed into the slot.

The tribute usually takes the most time, so settle its length first and fit the music around it. For a less traditional service, our guide to music for a celebration of life has ideas that suit a crematorium.

Frequently asked questions

Can you have live music at a crematorium?

Yes. Crematoria allow live musicians. A soloist or a choir of four suits the space: they lead the hymns, sing a piece during the reflection, and can sing as people come in and leave. Tell your funeral director when you book.

How long is a crematorium service?

Usually 30 to 40 minutes, set by the crematorium. Mortlake gives 40 minutes on weekdays, about 30 of them for the service; the City of London Crematorium gives 30 minutes before 10am and 40 minutes after. Both offer a double slot of 80 minutes at extra cost.

What music works best at a crematorium?

Two hymns and one solo fit a 30-minute service comfortably, with entry and exit music either side. Choose well-known hymns the congregation will feel confident singing, and settle the length of the tribute before adding more music.

Can I play my own song at a crematorium?

Yes. Crematoria play recordings through a chapel music system such as Obitus or Wesley Music, and your funeral director orders the tracks from its library. A piece not in the library can usually be ordered in advance, so give the funeral director your choices early.

Does a crematorium have an organ?

It depends on the chapel. Mortlake has a pipe organ; others have a digital organ, a keyboard or recorded music only. Your funeral director can tell you. We can send an organist for £250, or bring a portable keyboard where the chapel has no instrument.

Can you book a longer crematorium service?

Yes. Many crematoria sell a double slot, booked through your funeral director at extra cost. Mortlake suggests one if you expect more than 75 people or several tributes, and it gives you room for a third hymn or a second solo.
