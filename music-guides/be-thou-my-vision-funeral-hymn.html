Why Be Thou My Vision is the best funeral hymn

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 18 March 2026 · Updated 25 October 2026

Be Thou My Vision is an Irish hymn sung to the folk tune Slane, and families choose it for funerals in churches and crematoria. The words are a short prayer of trust, and the melody stays in a range most people can manage, so the congregation can keep singing when voices catch.

Is Be Thou My Vision suitable for a funeral? Yes. It is a prayer of trust that makes no promise grief will pass, and its tune, Slane, is easy for a congregation to follow. We suggest it as the last hymn, before the commendation and farewell. It also works as the first hymn, at a church funeral or a crematorium service of 30 to 40 minutes.

We sing it at funerals with a soloist, an organist or a choir. See our funeral singers page for what we provide, often at a few days’ notice.

The words offer real comfort without cliché

The hymn opens with one request: “Be thou my vision, O Lord of my heart.” It asks God to stay close and says nothing about how anyone ought to feel. At a funeral, mourners can sing it without being told their grief should already be easing.

The verses move from “my vision” through “my wisdom” and “my treasure” to the “High King of heaven” of the last verse. The early verses stay with the person singing. The last one looks beyond this life, so the hymn ends on hope without forcing it.

It is a Christian prayer, addressed to God from the first line to the last. At a church or crematorium service led by a minister it fits without question. At a humanist or non-religious ceremony the celebrant decides whether hymns are sung, so check with them before you include it.

The melody carries people through

The tune, Slane, is named after the Hill of Slane in County Meath. It is a folk melody whose phrases come back, so a congregation finds its way by the second verse, and it has no high climax to strain for.

At a funeral, voices break and some people stop singing for a line or two. Slane moves at an unhurried pace with room to breathe between phrases, and anyone who drops out can pick it up again at the next line.

Compare the other hymns families shortlist. Abide With Me builds towards its last verses and asks more of the voice. The Lord’s My Shepherd is so familiar that some people find it hard to get through. How Great Thou Art needs a confident voice for the refrain. Be Thou My Vision asks less of the congregation than any of them.

A trained singer at the front gives the congregation a line to follow, which helps most in the verses where voices drop away. We can lead the hymn at your service.

It suits every kind of funeral

Be Thou My Vision works in a large parish church and in a crematorium chapel with twenty people. It appears in Anglican and Catholic hymn books, so it suits a Church of England funeral or a Requiem Mass, and it fits a service for someone who went to church every week or hardly at all.

The Old Irish poem, Rop tú mo baile, is traditionally attributed to Dallán Forgaill, a poet of the sixth century, though scholars date the text to the tenth or eleventh century. Mary Byrne translated it into English prose in 1905 for the journal Ériu, and Eleanor Hull turned that prose into rhymed verse in 1912, in The Poem-Book of the Gael. Hull’s words are the ones sung today.

The tune came separately. Patrick Joyce published it in 1909 in Old Irish Folk Music and Songs, as the air of a love song called “With My Love on the Road”. Words and tune were first printed together in the Irish Church Hymnal of 1919. For families with Irish roots the hymn has a link to home, though you need no Irish connection to choose it.

Source: “Be Thou My Vision”, Wikipedia, which cites Byrne’s and Hull’s publications and Gerard Murphy’s dating of the Irish text.

It sounds beautiful with live musicians

A congregation can sing Be Thou My Vision with an organ alone. With singers at the front, people who are struggling hear the tune held for them and can rejoin when they are ready.

We usually take the first verse in unison, so the melody is clear, then sing in harmony from the second verse and add a descant over the last. A soloist can lead the hymn alone; with a choir of four, the congregation sings inside full harmony. You can hear our singers perform it on our listening page.

In Greater London a soloist costs from £275, a soloist and organist £550, and a choir of four from £1,150, with travel added outside London. Our funeral music page explains what we provide, and our pricing page lists every option.

Where it goes in the service

We suggest Be Thou My Vision as the last hymn, sung before the commendation and farewell. Its final verse turns towards hope, and the congregation then stands for the words of goodbye.

It also works as the first hymn, after the minister’s opening words, where its slow pace settles people who have just come in. A crematorium service usually runs 30 to 40 minutes and has room for two hymns; Be Thou My Vision fits either place. Our guide to choosing funeral music covers the rest of the order of service.

It sits well alongside other funeral music

A funeral usually has two or three hymns. Be Thou My Vision pairs with Abide With Me, which carries the farewell, and with The Lord’s My Shepherd, a psalm setting with a different pace.

Next to music the congregation listens to, such as an Ave Maria, In Paradisum or a song that meant something to the person who has died, the folk tune keeps its own character and does not clash with either.

Frequently asked questions

Is Be Thou My Vision suitable for a funeral?

Yes. It is a short prayer of trust in God, sung to the Irish folk tune Slane, and a congregation can follow it even when voices are unsteady. It suits church funerals, Requiem Masses and crematorium services.

Where does Be Thou My Vision go in a funeral service?

We suggest it as the last hymn, before the commendation and farewell, where its final verse ends the singing on hope. It also works as the first hymn, after the minister’s opening words. At a crematorium service of 30 to 40 minutes, it fits either of the two hymn places.

Who wrote Be Thou My Vision?

The Old Irish text is traditionally attributed to Dallán Forgaill, a sixth-century poet, though scholars date it to the tenth or eleventh century. Mary Byrne translated it into English prose in 1905, and Eleanor Hull put that translation into verse in 1912. Hull’s version is the one sung today.

What tune is Be Thou My Vision sung to?

Slane, an Irish folk melody named after the Hill of Slane in County Meath. Patrick Joyce published it in 1909, and it was first paired with the words in 1919. Its phrases repeat and it has no high climax, so people can keep singing when their voices falter.

Is Be Thou My Vision suitable for a non-religious funeral?

Is Be Thou My Vision a wedding hymn or a funeral hymn?

Both. Its words of devotion and trust suit a wedding and a funeral, and we sing it at both. Our separate guide covers Be Thou My Vision as a wedding hymn.
