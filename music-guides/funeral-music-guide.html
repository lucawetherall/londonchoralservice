How to choose music for a funeral

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 28 February 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

Most funerals have four to six pieces of music: two or three hymns, one piece the congregation sits and listens to, and something played as people arrive and as they leave. This guide takes each in turn, then the practical questions — the building, the timing and the cost — that settle many choices before taste does.

How do you choose music for a funeral? Start with what the person loved or sang, then fit it to the service. A church funeral usually has two or three hymns, one solo or choral piece, and music before and after. A crematorium slot of 30 to 40 minutes fits two hymns and one solo piece. Check with the minister what the building allows.

Start with the person

A lifelong churchgoer will have had hymns they loved. Someone who listened to Radio 3 every morning may have talked about one piece for years. One family asked us for Always Look on the Bright Side of Life as the music to leave by, because their father had quoted it at every opportunity, and the room laughed on the way out.

If nothing comes to mind, that is common, and the shape of the service will narrow the choice for you. Ask what the person would have wanted to hear rather than what seems proper for a funeral. Were they quiet or loud? Did they like tradition, or would they have wanted something of their own?

Hymns

Hymns give the congregation something to do together. A church funeral usually has two or three, and a crematorium service often two. The minister will suggest where they go, typically at the start, after a reading, and near the end.

The hymns families choose most are the ones people half-remember from school:

  • The Lord’s My Shepherd. Psalm 23 in the metrical version of the 1650 Scottish Psalter, sung to Crimond, a tune usually credited to Jessie Seymour Irvine (1872).
  • Abide with Me. Words by Henry Francis Lyte (1847), sung to William Henry Monk’s tune Eventide (1861).
  • Dear Lord and Father of Mankind. From a poem by John Greenleaf Whittier (1872), sung to Hubert Parry’s tune Repton.
  • The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended. Words by John Ellerton (1870), sung to St Clement. It was sung at Queen Elizabeth II’s funeral in 2022, as was The Lord’s My Shepherd.

Our guide to the most popular funeral hymns has notes on each. Fewer families choose hymns than a generation ago: in Co-op Funeralcare’s 2019 survey of its funeral directors, not one hymn made the top ten most requested funeral songs, and My Way came first.

Congregations sing hesitantly at funerals. People are upset, unsure of the tune, and nobody wants to be the loudest voice in the room. A trained singer leading from the front gives everyone the note and the confidence to join in, and a choir of four fills a parish church. Our guide to hiring a choir covers what professional singers do on the day.

A soloist costs from £250 and leads every hymn as well as singing up to three pieces. A soloist with an organist is £500, and a choir of four from £1,150, in Greater London.

Solo and choral pieces

These are the moments when the congregation sits and listens. The pieces families ask us for most are Schubert’s Ave Maria, Fauré’s Pie Jesu, Franck’s Panis Angelicus, and In Paradisum from Fauré’s Requiem. Ave Maria is warm and familiar, and people with no Catholic connection know it. Pie Jesu is a direct prayer for rest. In Paradisum is the lightest of the four, a long, high melody over a moving organ part, and it suits the end of a service.

Beyond those, we often sing Bruckner’s Locus Iste, Rutter’s God Be in My Head, and Steal Away arranged for solo voice, and Elgar’s Nimrod works on the organ or for string quartet. Finzi, Vaughan Williams and Parry all set English poems that suit a quieter farewell.

A celebration of life opens the range further, to popular songs, jazz standards and folk music. Our guide to the most popular funeral songs covers the non-hymn choices families make most often, and you can hear several of these pieces on our listening page. Instrumental music

Instrumental music covers the parts of the service nobody plans around: people arriving, the coffin coming in, a moment of reflection, people leaving.

The organ is still the usual choice in church. An organist sets the tone before the service starts and holds the congregation together in the hymns. Instruments vary: a large organ in a Westminster church sounds nothing like an upright piano in a small chapel in Lewisham. At a crematorium a piano often suits the room better, and most crematoria have one.

A harp suits a sacred or a secular service, and a string quartet covers Bach, Barber and Elgar. We have also provided guitarists, a jazz pianist and a small folk group where a family wanted the music to sound like the person.

You can still play a recording of a particular song, the person’s favourite record, say, alongside live music. The funeral director will know the venue’s sound system, and we plan the live music around the recorded piece.

Practical considerations

Venue. A parish church with a pipe organ and a long acoustic is a different proposition from a modern crematorium with an electronic keyboard. We sing in churches, crematoria and chapels across London, from Camden and Islington to Greenwich and Richmond. Before you settle on a piece, ask whether the venue has an organ in working order, whether there is room for musicians, and whether the crematorium allows live music (almost all do). The funeral director will know most of the answers, and our guide to music for a crematorium service covers the shorter setting.

Timing. A crematorium service usually has a slot of 30 to 40 minutes, sometimes less; a church funeral runs 45 minutes to an hour. Two hymns and one solo piece fit a crematorium slot. A church service has room for three hymns, a solo or choral piece, and music before and after.

Who to talk to. The funeral director runs the logistics of the day, and the minister or celebrant shapes the order of service. Once you book us, we deal with the funeral director directly, so the family has one fewer set of calls to make. Our services page explains how booking works.

Cost. A soloist costs from £250, a soloist and organist £500, and a choir of four from £1,150, with travel added outside Greater London. Every price is on our pricing page, and our guide to how much funeral music costs goes into more detail.

Frequently asked questions

How do you choose music for a funeral?

Start with what the person loved or sang, then fit it to the service. A church funeral usually has two or three hymns, one solo or choral piece, and music before and after. A crematorium slot of 30 to 40 minutes fits two hymns and one solo piece. Check with the minister what the building allows.

How many songs are played at a funeral?

Four to six pieces is usual. A church funeral often has two or three hymns, one solo or choral piece, and music as people arrive and leave. A crematorium service of 30 to 40 minutes fits two hymns and one solo piece, with music before and after.

How many hymns are sung at a funeral?

Two or three at a church funeral, and two at a shorter crematorium service. They usually come at the beginning, after a reading, and near the end.

What are the most popular funeral hymns?

The Lord’s My Shepherd (to the tune Crimond), Abide with Me, Dear Lord and Father of Mankind, and The Day Thou Gavest, Lord, Is Ended. Families choose them because people know the tunes well enough to sing when their voices are unsteady.

Can I have modern songs at a funeral?

Yes. Popular songs, jazz standards and folk music all suit a funeral, and a celebration of life most of all. Many families mix a live hymn or classical piece with a recording of a favourite song. In Co-op Funeralcare’s 2019 poll of its funeral directors, My Way was the most requested funeral song.
