The best Christmas carols for a carol service

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 4 March 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

Get the carols right and a room of three hundred sings without being asked twice. A good programme sets the congregational favourites that fill a church against the choir pieces that quieten it, and usually finds room for one piece most of the audience has never heard.

Which carols make the best carol service, and how many? Plan six to eight carols for a service of 45 minutes to an hour. Give the congregation three or four it knows, such as Once in Royal David’s City, O Come, All Ye Faithful and Hark! The Herald Angels Sing. Add two or three choir pieces, such as Darke’s In the Bleak Midwinter, and finish with a big congregational carol.

Planning one this December? We supply the choir and organist for church carol services, and carol singers for offices, hotels and parties.

The same principles apply to a corporate carol service, an office Christmas event and a traditional service of Nine Lessons and Carols. These are the carols we come back to, and what each one is for.

Carols everyone knows

Your congregation will sing these without coaxing. Put at least three or four in the programme, at the points where you want the whole room singing.

O Come, All Ye Faithful

The Latin hymn Adeste Fideles is usually credited to John Francis Wade, whose 1751 publication is the earliest printed source. Most British congregations sing Frederick Oakeley’s English translation of 1841. The melody is bold and sits in a comfortable range for most voices. Everyone knows the first verse in English, and plenty of people will try the Latin if you print it. Each verse builds towards the refrain, “O come, let us adore him”, and a full church sings it at full voice. Use it to open the service, or to send people out into the cold at the end.

Hark! The Herald Angels Sing

Charles Wesley’s words first appeared in 1739. The tune comes from Mendelssohn’s Festgesang of 1840, a secular piece for the 400th anniversary of Gutenberg’s printing press, and William Hayman Cummings fitted it to Wesley’s text in 1855. The melody climbs through each verse, and a descant on the last verse gives the choir a line above the congregation. People who say they can’t sing join in with this one. Place it early in the service, or keep it for the last carol, as King’s College, Cambridge does.

Once in Royal David’s City

Cecil Frances Alexander published the words in 1848, and Henry Gauntlett wrote the tune, Irby, a year later. It has been the processional at the Festival of Nine Lessons and Carols at King’s College, Cambridge since 1919. At King’s a boy chorister sings the first verse unaccompanied, the choir sings the second, and the congregation joins from the third. That build from one voice to a full church makes a strong opening. You need a soloist who can start cold, unaccompanied, in a silent building, and that is harder than it sounds. Our Christmas choir hire covers that first verse.

Silent Night

Franz Xaver Gruber wrote the tune to Joseph Mohr’s words, and the two men first performed it in the Nikolauskirche at Oberndorf, near Salzburg, on Christmas Eve 1818. Use it for the quiet point in the middle of the service, after something louder. Congregations sing it softly. If the venue can dim the lights for this one carol, ask. Put the choir into harmony on the later verses and let the congregation hum underneath.

O Little Town of Bethlehem

Phillips Brooks, an American priest, wrote the words in 1868. In Britain congregations sing them to Forest Green, which Ralph Vaughan Williams adapted from a Surrey folk song for the English Hymnal of 1906. It needs less volume than O Come, All Ye Faithful, so you can use it to lower the temperature and give the programme contrast. Put it in the middle of the service, between two livelier items.

Away in a Manger

In Britain the familiar tune is William J. Kirkpatrick’s Cradle Song, published in 1895; American congregations use a different tune, known as Mueller. Sung by a congregation alone it can sound thin, and a choir singing it in four parts gives it body. Its lullaby rhythm suits a family service, or any point where you want the volume to drop.

Sources: O Come, All Ye Faithful, Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, Once in Royal David’s City, Silent Night, O Little Town of Bethlehem and Away in a Manger (Wikipedia).

Carols that shine as choir pieces

Some of the best moments in a carol service come when the congregation sits and listens to something it could not sing itself. These carols need trained voices and a quiet room.

In the Bleak Midwinter

Christina Rossetti’s poem was first published in 1872, and two settings dominate. Gustav Holst’s tune, Cranham, appeared in the English Hymnal in 1906; its plain, folk-like line is the one most congregations know. Harold Darke wrote his anthem in 1909, while he was a student at the Royal College of Music, and Stainer & Bell published it in 1911. Darke’s setting is longer, with solo verses, richer harmony and a quiet final verse. If your choir can sing the Darke, it will be the piece people remember from the evening.

O Holy Night

Adolphe Adam composed it in 1847 to Placide Cappeau’s French poem, Minuit, chrétiens; the English words most people know are John Sullivan Dwight’s, from 1855. It is operatic and needs a strong soloist, someone with the range for the climax on “O night divine” who will not strain at the top. Sung well, it is the high point of the evening; sung badly, it is painful. Book a professional singer for it, and expect it to be requested: clients at the corporate carol services we sing at ask for it often.

Sussex Carol

Luke Wadding printed the words in 1684. Vaughan Williams took down the tune from Harriet Verrall of Monk’s Gate, near Horsham in Sussex, and published it in 1919; it is the tune sung today. In a four-part arrangement such as David Willcocks’s, from the first Carols for Choirs, it is bright and quick, and it wakes an audience up after something slow.

Ding Dong! Merrily on High

George Ratcliffe Woodward wrote the words for The Cambridge Carol-Book of 1924, to a French dance tune, Branle de l’Official, from Thoinot Arbeau’s Orchésographie of 1589. A confident choir makes the long runs on “Gloria” ring. The verses trip congregations up, because the rhythm is harder than it looks, so give this one to the choir. Programme it near the end, to lift the energy before the last carol.

Less common carols worth discovering

A programme made only of favourites can feel predictable. One or two pieces your audience has not heard give the service its own character.

Coventry Carol

The carol belongs to the Pageant of the Shearmen and Tailors, one of the Coventry mystery plays. Robert Croo wrote down the text in 1534, and the earliest surviving setting of the melody dates from 1591. In the play the women of Bethlehem sing it as a lullaby to children Herod has ordered killed, in the Massacre of the Innocents. It is in a minor key, with a famous Picardy third (a major chord closing a minor phrase). Among the major-key carols it gives the service a moment of grief.

Adam Lay Ybounden

The text survives in a British Library manuscript dated to around 1400. Boris Ord, organist of King’s College, Cambridge from 1929 to 1957, set it in 1957, and it is his best-known piece; King’s often sings it after the First Lesson at Nine Lessons and Carols. It is short and asks more of a choir than its length suggests: the melody looks simple, and the harmonies under it keep shifting. Programme it early, as a quiet opening to a set of choir pieces.

A Spotless Rose

Herbert Howells set Catherine Winkworth’s English text in 1919, as a motet for four-part choir. He said he wrote it after watching trains shunting from the window of a cottage in Gloucester. The harmonies are rich, the lines interweave, and the quiet ending is the hardest passage to bring off. It needs a choir that can sing softly and keep the line. Put it at the reflective point of the service, away from anything loud.

Tomorrow Shall Be My Dancing Day

The words first appeared in print in William Sandys’s Christmas Carols Ancient and Modern of 1833, though the carol is thought to be much older. John Gardner’s setting, for choir and organ with optional percussion, is fast and built on a dance rhythm that keeps all four parts moving. Audiences who have not heard it before tend to ask afterwards what it was.

Sources: Coventry Carol, Adam lay ybounden, A Spotless Rose and Tomorrow Shall Be My Dancing Day (Wikipedia).

Building a carol service programme

Plan a carol service as you would a concert, with shape, contrast and pacing. We follow these rules when we help clients plan theirs.

Open with something everyone knows. Get people singing from the first carol. Once in Royal David’s City gives an atmospheric start, O Come, All Ye Faithful an exuberant one.

Alternate energy levels. Follow a big congregational carol with a quieter choir piece, and a reflective moment with something bright. The changes keep people listening and give each item space.

Put readings between the carols. A service of Nine Lessons and Carols alternates Bible readings with carols, and the format works in a secular setting too. The readings give the congregation a rest from singing and tell the story in order; our guide to Nine Lessons and Carols lists the readings and the usual order of service. For a corporate event, you might use poems or seasonal prose in place of scripture.

End with the whole room singing. Finish on something rousing and familiar, usually O Come, All Ye Faithful or Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, and send people out singing.

Plan six to eight carols for a service of 45 minutes to an hour. Fewer than six and the programme feels thin; more than eight and it drags. Aim for three or four congregational carols, two or three choir pieces and one less familiar carol. A full Nine Lessons and Carols has a carol after each reading and runs longer, usually 55 to 70 minutes.

A sample programme for a 50-minute service with three readings:

  1. Once in Royal David’s City (solo first verse, congregation from the third)
  2. Welcome and first reading
  3. O Little Town of Bethlehem (congregation)
  4. Adam Lay Ybounden (choir)
  5. Second reading
  6. Sussex Carol (choir)
  7. Silent Night (congregation, lights down)
  8. Third reading
  9. Darke’s In the Bleak Midwinter (choir)
  10. Ding Dong! Merrily on High (choir)
  11. Hark! The Herald Angels Sing (congregation)

Getting your audience to sing

The rules are the same as for choosing wedding hymns: familiar tunes, a confident choir leading from the front, printed words, and a building where people can hear each other. A congregation sings when it knows the melody and hears the voices around it singing too.

Choose tunes people know. Some carols have more than one tune, and the wrong choice leaves a room of people mouthing along. If in doubt, use the version they sang at school.

Put a strong choir at the front. When people hear trained voices carrying the melody they join in; when they can’t, they mumble the second verse. Four singers will lead a room of 150, and eight will carry a church of 400. That is the main reason organisations hire a professional choir for a carol service: the congregational carols need a lead, and the choir pieces need the singers.

Print every verse. Few people know all the verses of every carol. Print the full words at a readable size in the order of service.

Give a clear introduction. Whether you have an organ, a piano or a brass group, people need to hear the tune before they sing it. A confident play-over sets the tempo and the key and tells people when to come in; without one, the first line falls apart.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best carols for a carol service?

For the congregation: Once in Royal David’s City, O Come, All Ye Faithful, Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, Silent Night, O Little Town of Bethlehem and Away in a Manger. For the choir alone: Darke’s In the Bleak Midwinter, Sussex Carol, Ding Dong! Merrily on High and, with a strong soloist, O Holy Night. Mix the two lists.

How many carols should a carol service have?

Six to eight for a service of 45 minutes to an hour: three or four congregational carols, two or three choir pieces and one less familiar carol. A full Nine Lessons and Carols has a carol after each of the nine readings and runs 55 to 70 minutes.

What is the most popular Christmas carol?

At British carol services, O Come, All Ye Faithful, Hark! The Herald Angels Sing and Once in Royal David’s City are the three we see on nearly every order of service. We open or close most of the services we sing at with one of them.

How do you put together a carol service programme?

Open with a carol everyone knows, such as Once in Royal David’s City. Alternate congregational carols with choir pieces, and put readings between them so people rest from singing. Place quiet carols such as Silent Night in the middle, a lively choir piece near the end, and finish with Hark! The Herald Angels Sing or O Come, All Ye Faithful.

Which carols should the choir sing on its own?

Pieces a congregation could not sing without rehearsal: Harold Darke’s In the Bleak Midwinter, Herbert Howells’s A Spotless Rose, Boris Ord’s Adam Lay Ybounden, the Coventry Carol and Ding Dong! Merrily on High. Two or three in a service of six to eight carols give contrast without leaving the congregation seated for long.

What should we budget for a carol service choir?

In Greater London a choir of four costs £1,150, six £1,600 and eight £2,000, and an organist adds £250. A booking covers the length of the service. Christmas Eve and Christmas Day carry a premium of around 25%, and travel is added outside London.
