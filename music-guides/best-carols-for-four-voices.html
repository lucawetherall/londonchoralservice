The best carols for four voices

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 31 July 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

Four singers, one to each part, is where our carol bookings start, and most published carol arrangements were written with a bigger choir in mind. Some pieces gain from the clarity of four voices; others fall apart. This guide sorts the standard repertoire into which is which.

What are the best carols for four voices (SATB)? Carols written or harmonised in plain four parts: Praetorius’s Es ist ein Ros entsprungen (1609), Charles Wood’s harmonisation of Ding Dong! Merrily on High (1924), Holst’s In the Bleak Midwinter (1906), Warlock’s Bethlehem Down (1927), Boris Ord’s Adam Lay Ybounden (1957) and the Sussex Carol. Darke’s In the Bleak Midwinter and the Willcocks descants sound better with six or eight.

Booking a quartet for carols? In Greater London a choir of four costs £1,150 for up to two hours, rehearsal included, and an organist is £250. Our carol services page has the other sizes.

What one voice per part changes

With four singers there is nowhere to hide. Every line is exposed, every entry is audible, and a single voice out of tune is not absorbed by the three others singing the same part. That cuts both ways: the texture is clearer than a larger choir can manage, and the mistakes are clearer too.

The practical consequences are worth knowing before you choose a programme. Divisi — where a part splits in two — is impossible without dropping a line. Long sustained passages are harder, because nobody can drop out to breathe without the harmony thinning. And anything relying on sheer weight of sound, the big Willcocks descant arrangements in particular, will sound underpowered however good the four singers are.

What four voices do better than eight is move. Rhythmic carols stay crisp, counterpoint stays audible, and the ensemble can follow a tempo change without a conductor.

Carols that suit four voices

Ding Dong! Merrily on High

The tune is a dance from Thoinot Arbeau’s Orchésographie (1589). George Ratcliffe Woodward wrote the words for The Cambridge Carol-Book (1924), and Charles Wood harmonised it for that book. The cascading Gloria refrain makes the parts chase each other, and with one voice per line you can hear the chase. In a larger choir it turns into a wash.

Eight of our singers performing Ding Dong! Merrily on High. Hear more recordings.

The Sussex Carol

Fast, rhythmic, and dependent on the inner parts being audible. Four voices keep it light on its feet. Vaughan Williams took the tune down from Harriet Verrall of Monk’s Gate, near Horsham, and published it in 1919; David Willcocks’s arrangement in Carols for Choirs 1 (1961) is the one King’s College sings. Most quartets sing a plain four-part harmonisation, and the carol loses nothing by it.

Adam Lay Ybounden

Boris Ord’s setting (1957), his only published composition, lasts under two minutes and is written for SATB. King’s sings it after the first lesson. It is quiet, exposed writing, which suits a group that can hear each other properly. Programme it as the still point of a set.

In the Bleak Midwinter (Holst)

Holst wrote the tune, Cranham, for The English Hymnal of 1906. It is a hymn in four parts, and a quartet sings it as written. Harold Darke’s setting of 1909 is a different matter: it is an anthem with solos for soprano and tenor, and it wants more than one voice on the top line.

Bethlehem Down

Peter Warlock set Bruce Blunt’s poem for unaccompanied SATB in 1927, and it won that year’s Daily Telegraph carol competition. The harmony is rich and slow, and one voice to a part lets every suspension be heard. It needs four singers who tune well together.

Coventry Carol

The words survive in Robert Croo’s manuscript of 1534, and Thomas Mawdyke added the music in 1591, in three parts. The four-part arrangements choirs sing today keep that spareness. A large choir makes it sound thicker than it should, which is why it suits a quartet.

Gaudete

The text comes from Piae Cantiones, printed in 1582. Unaccompanied, rhythmic, and effective with four voices in a resonant space. It also works outdoors better than most, because it does not depend on quiet dynamics surviving the open air.

Four more that reward a quartet

Es ist ein Ros entsprungen (Praetorius)

The Praetorius harmonisation is four-part writing from 1609 and has never needed anything added to it. Quiet, slow, and exposed, which is why it is a good test of whether four singers are actually listening to each other. Sing it in German or in English (Theodore Baker’s Lo, How a Rose E’er Blooming of 1894 is the usual translation), but not in both halves of the same programme.

Personent Hodie

Loud, rhythmic and medieval, from the same 1582 Piae Cantiones as Gaudete, and the four-part version keeps the drive that larger arrangements tend to smooth out. It is a useful opener for a set where you want the audience to stop talking without being asked.

The Holly and the Ivy

Deceptively plain. The traditional four-part harmonisation is well within a quartet, and its restraint makes a good contrast next to anything showier. Congregations will also join in on it, so it can sit on either side of the line depending on how you introduce it.

Tomorrow Shall Be My Dancing Day

John Gardner’s setting (1965) gives the parts enough independence that four voices can carry it, and the rhythm stays tighter with one on a line than with three. It needs confidence rather than numbers. It also needs a piano, so book a pianist with the quartet.

Carols that want more than four

O Holy Night needs a soloist who can carry the climax, and a quartet leaves nobody to accompany them properly. Hark! The Herald Angels Sing with the Willcocks descant needs a treble line strong enough to sit above a congregation, which one soprano cannot do. Howells’s A Spotless Rose (1919) sets a baritone solo against unaccompanied SATB, so with four singers the choir loses its bass whenever the soloist sings. Many of the larger twentieth-century carol anthems divide the parts and want a choral sound rather than a consort one.

None of these are impossible with four voices. They are better with six or eight, and if your programme leans on them, size up rather than compromise the pieces.

Accompanied carols are a separate question. John Rutter’s Shepherd’s Pipe Carol, from Carols for Choirs 2 (1970), sits in four parts but needs a pianist or organist as well as the voices. A pianist or organist is £250 added to a choir.

Carols the room can sing over four voices

A quartet leading congregational singing is doing a different job from a quartet performing. The carols that work are the ones where the melody is strong enough to carry a room on its own: O Come, All Ye Faithful, Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, Once in Royal David’s City, Silent Night.

Four voices will lead a room of around 150 in these. Beyond that, the congregation starts to lead itself, which is fine until a verse goes wrong and there is not enough choir to pull it back.

Print the words. A quartet cannot rescue a congregation that does not know the third verse.

Christmas songs, as opposed to carols

Most Christmas bookings want some material that is not a carol at all: the songs people associate with December rather than with Advent. Four voices handle these well, because close-harmony arrangements of popular songs were mostly written for exactly that texture.

Have Yourself a Merry Little Christmas and The Christmas Song sit naturally in four parts and suit a quiet room. Winter Wonderland and Let It Snow want a bit of swing and work better standing than roaming, because the rhythm needs the four singers watching each other. White Christmas is the one clients ask for most and the one that most needs a soloist on the melody with the other three underneath, rather than four-square harmony throughout.

Our guide to a cappella Christmas songs covers this repertoire in more detail.

Where to stand

Four singers should stand in a close arc, facing slightly inward, close enough to hear each other without effort. A quartet spread across a stage in a line will not stay together, because each singer is hearing the others late.

Put them where the room helps: a hard floor, a corner, the foot of a staircase, under a gallery. Height above the group matters more than distance from the audience. A quartet at the bottom of a stairwell will fill a building; the same quartet in the middle of a carpeted room will not reach the back of it.

For congregational carols, the singers need to be seen as well as heard. A congregation takes its timing visually more than people realise, which is why a choir tucked behind a pillar leads worse than the same choir standing in front of it.

Building an hour for four voices

Six to eight carols fills forty-five minutes to an hour with readings between them. For a quartet, a workable shape is three or four congregational carols, three choir-only pieces chosen from the list above, and one piece most of the audience will not know. Our guide to the best carols for a carol service covers the congregational list and the order to put it in.

Alternate the energy. A fast unaccompanied piece after a big congregational carol gives the room a change of texture, and gives four singers a rest from projecting. Four voices tire faster than eight, because nobody can drop out for a verse.

Open with something the room knows. A quartet has less margin than a larger choir for a shaky start, and a first carol everyone can sing covers any settling the group needs to do.

If there is an organ in the building, use it on the congregational carols and let the quartet sing the choir pieces unaccompanied. That combination covers more ground than either on its own, and an organist is £250.

Arrangements and requests

Requests are usually workable if they arrive in time. A carol we already sing needs no notice. A song that has to be arranged for four parts needs a couple of weeks, and the arrangement has to be written for one voice per line rather than adapted from a choral edition, which is a different job.

Send requests when you book rather than in the week before. December rehearsal time is the scarcest thing we have, and a piece that arrives late is a piece the quartet sight-reads.

Frequently asked questions

Can four singers cover a full carol service?

Yes. Four voices in soprano, alto, tenor and bass cover the standard carol repertoire and will lead a congregation of around 150. Pieces written with divisi, or the bigger descant arrangements, are better with six or eight.

Which Christmas carols work best with one voice per part?

Ding Dong! Merrily on High, the Sussex Carol, Adam Lay Ybounden, the Coventry Carol, Gaudete, and Holst's setting of In the Bleak Midwinter. All sit well in the four-part arrangements most choirs sing, and the clarity of a quartet helps rather than hinders them.

Do you need a soloist as well as four singers?

Not usually. One of the four takes any solo line, which is how Once in Royal David's City is normally sung. A separate soloist is worth adding when a piece needs a voice to sit above the ensemble throughout, such as O Holy Night.

What does SATB mean?

Soprano, alto, tenor and bass, the four parts of a standard choral arrangement. A choir of four sings SATB with one voice on each part; a choir of eight has two on each. Most published carol arrangements are written in these four parts, and some divide into more.

Can four singers sing Darke’s In the Bleak Midwinter?

They can, but it is not the best choice. Darke wrote it in 1909 as an anthem for choir and organ with soprano and tenor solos, and the full verses want more than one voice on the top line. Holst’s 1906 tune suits a quartet better; for the Darke, book six or eight singers and an organist.

How much does a quartet cost for a carol service?

In Greater London a choir of four costs £1,150 for up to two hours, including rehearsal. An organist or pianist is £250, a choir of six £1,600 and a choir of eight £2,000. Travel beyond Greater London is quoted with the booking.
