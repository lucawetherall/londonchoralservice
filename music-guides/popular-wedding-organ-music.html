Popular wedding organ music

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 6 March 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

The organ pieces couples book most often at British weddings, in service order: processionals for the walk in, quieter pieces for the signing of the register, and recessionals for the way out. After those come four rarer pieces worth asking your organist for, the pop songs that suit the instrument, and a sample four-piece programme.

What is the most popular wedding organ music? For the walk in, Jeremiah Clarke’s Trumpet Voluntary (c.1700), Pachelbel’s Canon in D and Wagner’s Bridal Chorus (1850). For the signing of the register, Bach’s Jesu, Joy of Man’s Desiring. For the way out, Mendelssohn’s Wedding March (1842) and the Toccata from Widor’s Organ Symphony No. 5 (1879). Couples ask us for these six more than any others.

The five processionals couples book most

The processional is the piece couples choose first, and these five take most of our bookings.

Trumpet Voluntary (Jeremiah Clarke, c.1700)

Its real title is the Prince of Denmark’s March. Clarke, the first organist of the rebuilt St Paul’s Cathedral, published it in a keyboard collection around 1700, and for years it was credited to Henry Purcell because of a Victorian organ arrangement that named him. Lady Diana Spencer walked up the aisle of St Paul’s to it in 1981. The opening trumpet figure suits a stone church and a steady walking pace, and the piece holds together on a small village organ as well as a cathedral instrument, which is why so many of our wedding enquiries arrive with it already chosen.

Bridal Chorus (Wagner, 1850)

The “Here Comes the Bride” tune from Lohengrin, first staged in 1850. Some couples rule it out as cliché; others pick it because their grandparents recognise it. It divides opinion more than the other four pieces here. A skilled organist plays it with enough weight and dignity to lift it above its cartoon reputation.

Canon in D (Pachelbel, late 17th century)

Slower than the Trumpet Voluntary, with a bass line that circles the same eight-note ground. It suits a slower walk and a smaller church. We often pair it with strings for couples who want the violins on the melody and the organ underneath. The repeating ground bass has a practical use too: the organist can stretch or shorten the piece on the day to fit the length of your walk.

Arrival of the Queen of Sheba (Handel, 1748)

From the oratorio Solomon. A brighter alternative if Pachelbel and Clarke feel too heavy: quick, light and full of forward momentum. The two oboe lines move to flute stops on the organ, and the piece is short enough to time the walk down a long aisle without rushing.

Prelude in C from The Well-Tempered Clavier (Bach, BWV 846, 1722)

The opening prelude of the first book of The Well-Tempered Clavier, which most keyboard students have practised at some point. Its rolling arpeggios sound restrained, almost private, on a soft organ. Choose it if you want the entrance to feel intimate rather than ceremonial. It works in a smaller church but can sound thin in a cathedral, where a quiet keyboard texture struggles to fill the space.

If one of these is on your shortlist, we can send you a recording of an organ arrangement before you decide.

Four pieces that suit the register signing

You sit at a side table with your witnesses for somewhere between four and eight minutes while the minister or registrar completes the register. The organist plays through the pause so the room doesn’t sit in silence. This is also a natural moment for a choir or soloist to sing, but the organ can fill it alone.

Jesu, Joy of Man’s Desiring (Bach, 1723)

Bach wrote this chorale setting for his cantata BWV 147. It is the safest choice for the register signing: a melody most guests recognise, over a flowing triplet accompaniment that holds attention without demanding it. It carries the pause whether your guests are listening or chatting. Pick this one if you cannot decide.

Air on the G String (Bach, c.1730)

The slow second movement of the Orchestral Suite No. 3, transcribed for organ. Quieter and slower-moving than Jesu, Joy, closer to a long held breath than a tune. It suits a couple who want guests to settle into stillness during the pause.

Gymnopédie No. 1 (Satie, 1888)

An unexpected choice for a church wedding. Satie wrote it for piano in 1888, and its spare, slightly wistful writing still sounds modern. On a soft flute stop it is delicate and intimate. Couples who choose it usually want the ceremony to sound a little different from the standard programme.

Clair de Lune (Debussy, 1905)

Originally for piano, the third movement of the Suite bergamasque, published in 1905. The organ transcription needs a skilled player: on the piano the dynamic shading comes from the fingertips, and on the organ the player has to manage it through stops and the swell box. When it works, it is the most distinctive register-signing piece on this list. Ask your organist whether they have it under their fingers before you commit.

Six recessionals that lift the room

The recessional is the loudest, brightest piece of music in the ceremony. The doors open, the guests applaud, and the organist gets to use most of the instrument.

Imperial March (Elgar, 1897)

Elgar composed the Imperial March for Queen Victoria’s Diamond Jubilee, and it brings that ceremonial weight into the church. It is the slowest and grandest of the recessionals here, with a stately tread that needs the full organ from the first bar. Choose it if you want the moment the doors open to feel like a state occasion, and add brass if your budget allows. It will not come off on a small two-manual instrument, so check what your church has first.

Wedding March (Mendelssohn, 1842)

From the incidental music to A Midsummer Night’s Dream. The most-requested recessional on this list, and one of the few pieces here that needs full pedal and reeds. Guests hear the opening chords and know the ceremony is over. Add brass if your budget allows; on a good organ it works alone.

Toccata from Symphony No. 5 (Widor, 1879)

Widor wrote the symphony while he was organist of Saint-Sulpice in Paris and gave its first public performance in 1879. The Toccata drew wide attention as a wedding piece when Princess Margaret left Westminster Abbey to it in 1960, in place of the Mendelssohn. Faster than Mendelssohn and more virtuosic, it needs a confident player and an organ with sparkle in its upper voices, so ask your organist whether the piece suits both.

Hornpipe from Water Music (Handel, 1717)

Handel wrote the Water Music for George I’s barge trip on the Thames in July 1717. The Hornpipe’s bouncing rhythm carries a recessional with or without brass. It is lighter than Widor and less ceremonial than Walton or Elgar, and couples pick it when they want the exit to feel like the start of a party.

Crown Imperial (Walton, 1937)

Composed for the coronation of George VI. The marching pulse gives it more weight than Mendelssohn, with a swagger the Widor lacks, and it moves more lightly than the Elgar. A strong option if you want the recessional to feel ceremonial. It sounds best in a large parish church or a cathedral.

Toccata in F (Bach, BWV 540)

Less well known than the Widor and harder to play. The hands play a long canon over a sustained pedal note, then the feet take a solo of their own, and the pattern repeats in a new key before the music works its way home to F. It is one of the longest preludes Bach wrote, so organists usually play the toccata alone and leave out the fugue. Choose it if you want something virtuosic and your organist relishes a challenge; ask them first.

Rarer pieces to ask for

Most couples stick to the well-known pieces, and that is a sound choice. If you want something less expected, these four are rarely requested, so your guests are unlikely to have heard them at another wedding.

Prélude from Te Deum (Charpentier). You know the tune even if you don’t know the name: it is the Eurovision theme. On a full organ it is bright and festive, and it works as a processional or a recessional.

Allegro maestoso from the Organ Sonata in G (Elgar, 1895). Elgar wrote this sonata for the organ itself, and the first movement has the breadth of his orchestral music. It builds steadily and suits a processional that should feel significant without showing off.

Final from Symphony No. 1 (Vierne). A French Romantic showpiece that rivals the Widor Toccata for excitement and is played far less often. Dramatic and virtuosic as a recessional.

Nimrod (Elgar, organ transcription). The ninth of the Enigma Variations (1899) is not an obvious wedding choice, but it can move a congregation during the register signing. It grows from a whisper to a full climax and falls away again, and on a good organ the climax fills the building.

If any of these appeal, mention them to your organist.

Pop songs and film themes that work on the organ

The organ handles most pop ballads better than the studio version suggests. Songs with strong melodies and moving harmony transfer directly; anything that depends on percussion or vocal phrasing tends to fall short. The four below come up most often at our weddings, and our guide to pop, film and musical theatre on the organ covers the longer list with arrangement notes for each piece.

  • Can’t Help Falling in Love (Elvis Presley, 1961). Its songwriters based the melody on an eighteenth-century French song, Martini’s “Plaisir d’amour”, which is why it works on the organ at all. A good organist opens on soft flute stops and builds the final verse to something close to a hymn.
  • A Thousand Years (Christina Perri, 2011). The piano arpeggios in the original sit straight under the organist’s hands, and the chorus opens up well on a principal stop. A natural register-signing or processional choice.
  • All You Need Is Love (The Beatles, 1967). The Beatles built the intro on the opening of “La Marseillaise”, so the piece already sounds like processional music. Bold and joyful as a recessional with full stops out.
  • Game of Thrones main title (Ramin Djawadi, 2011). The cello line moves to the pedals and the strings to the manuals, and the result fills a stone church. It suits couples who want the entrance to feel dramatic rather than romantic.

Which of these is right for your wedding

Four questions to narrow the list.

How long is the walk down the aisle?

A long aisle of twenty paces or more needs a piece with internal repeats, so the organist can lengthen or shorten it on the day. Trumpet Voluntary, Canon in D and Bach’s Prelude in C all allow this. The Bridal Chorus does not: it runs to a fixed length, and if your walk runs long the organist has to repeat the introduction or stretch the final cadence, which a musical guest will notice.

How big is the organ?

A small two-manual organ in a country church will not deliver the bigger pieces, and a small electronic instrument struggles with them too. Widor’s Toccata, Bach’s Toccata in F, Elgar’s Imperial March, the Mendelssohn Wedding March and Walton’s Crown Imperial all want at least a large three-manual instrument. On a smaller organ, Pachelbel’s Canon, Handel’s Hornpipe and the Trumpet Voluntary carry the moment without straining the stops. Ask your organist what their instrument can handle before you commit to a showpiece.

How does the building sound?

A resonant stone church sustains the sound and flatters slow pieces such as the Canon or Nimrod. A modern building with a dry acoustic suits music with more rhythmic drive, such as the Hornpipe or the Queen of Sheba. A good organist knows the instrument and the room and will steer you towards pieces that sound their best there; our guide to hiring a wedding organist covers how to find one.

Have either of you sung in a choir or studied music?

Couples who have sung in a choir or studied music sometimes gravitate to Bach’s Prelude in C, Walton’s Crown Imperial or the Elgar, where the writing rewards close listening. Couples without that background usually pick Trumpet Voluntary, Pachelbel or Mendelssohn. The popular choices are popular because they fit the moment, so picking what most people pick is rarely a mistake.

Building a four-piece programme

Most couples end up with four pieces of organ music: a prelude as guests arrive, a processional, something for the register signing, and a recessional. A working programme might look like this:

  • Prelude as guests arrive (around 15 minutes): A loose set of two or three pieces. Bach’s Prelude in C, Satie’s Gymnopédie No. 1 and Pachelbel’s Canon work together, and the organist can cut the set short the moment the bride arrives.
  • Processional: Trumpet Voluntary or Pachelbel’s Canon for most couples. Wagner if your family expects it. Bach’s Prelude in C if you want a quieter entrance.
  • Register signing: Jesu, Joy of Man’s Desiring is the safest pick. Air on the G String or the Satie if you want something more reflective. Clair de Lune if your organist is up for it.
  • Recessional: Mendelssohn or Widor for the full lift. Elgar’s Imperial March for state-occasion grandeur, Walton’s Crown Imperial for ceremonial weight, Bach’s Toccata in F for a virtuoso finish, or Handel’s Hornpipe for something lighter.

If a hymn or two sits between the readings and the vows, the organ programme above slots round them. Our guide to wedding ceremony music walks through the full running order, including hymns, blessings and the choir-only pieces.

Frequently asked questions

What organ music is played when the bride walks in?

Jeremiah Clarke’s Trumpet Voluntary, also called the Prince of Denmark’s March, is the usual choice at British weddings. Pachelbel’s Canon in D suits a slower walk, Wagner’s Bridal Chorus is the traditional “Here Comes the Bride”, and Handel’s Arrival of the Queen of Sheba is a brighter alternative.

What organ music is played as the couple leaves?

Mendelssohn’s Wedding March is the traditional recessional, and the Toccata from Widor’s Organ Symphony No. 5 is the main alternative. Couples who want something lighter choose the Hornpipe from Handel’s Water Music; those who want ceremony choose Walton’s Crown Imperial or Elgar’s Imperial March.

How many pieces of organ music do I need for a wedding?

Most couples choose four pieces: a prelude as guests arrive, a processional, something for the register signing, and a recessional. Many add one or two hymns on top. A larger programme of five or six pieces is fine but not required.

Is the Bridal Chorus still considered acceptable?

Yes. Wagner’s Bridal Chorus has fallen out of fashion with couples who want the ceremony to feel modern, but it remains a legitimate choice and couples still ask us for it. If a parent or grandparent would like it and you don’t object, use it.

Can my organist play a piece that isn’t on this list?

Yes. Give a professional organist eight weeks and a recording of the version you want, and the piece will be ready for the day. Four weeks is the minimum for a bespoke arrangement. If you book through The London Choral Service, we can arrange a piece for you; depending on the work involved this can carry an extra charge, which we quote before you book.
