Christmas carol lyrics: the words and what they mean

By Luca Wetherall, Artistic Director & Tutor in Music, University of Oxford

Published 31 July 2026 · Updated 24 October 2026

You can probably sing three verses of half a dozen carols without knowing what all the words mean. The texts are old, several are translations, and a few of the best-known lines mean something different from what they sound like. This guide takes the words of eleven carols in turn: what the puzzling phrases mean, who wrote each text and when.

What do the words of Christmas carols mean? Most carol texts use older senses of everyday words. In “God rest ye merry, gentlemen”, rest means keep, so the line asks God to keep you in good spirits. Welkin, in Charles Wesley’s 1739 original of Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, is the sky. Yon in Silent Night means that one over there, and figgy pudding was the West Country name for a plum pudding.

“God rest ye merry, gentlemen”

Carol sheets often put the comma in the wrong place, and moving it changes the sentence. Rest here is the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century verb meaning keep or cause to remain, and merry is the state God keeps you in: happy, in good heart. The line means “may God keep you in good spirits, gentlemen”, a blessing on the gentlemen rather than a description of them.

The next line, “let nothing you dismay”, carries the same blessing on: do not let anything trouble you. The carol is older than most on this page. An early version survives in a manuscript from the 1650s, and the first known printing is a broadside of about 1760.

Printed correctly it reads God rest ye merry, gentlemen. If your order of service has God rest ye, merry gentlemen, fix it before it goes to print.

“Hark! The Herald Angels Sing”

Charles Wesley published “Hark, how all the welkin rings” in 1739, in Hymns and Sacred Poems. Welkin is an old word for the sky, from the Old English for cloud, so the original line has the whole heavens ringing. George Whitefield changed the opening to the version we sing in his 1753 hymn collection. It is easier to sing and less interesting.

“Veiled in flesh the Godhead see” is the line that stops people. It is a compressed statement of the incarnation: God hidden inside a human body. The next line, “Hail th’incarnate Deity”, says the same thing more plainly.

Mendelssohn wrote the tune in 1840 for his Festgesang, a cantata for the four hundredth anniversary of printing, and wrote in a letter that it would never do for sacred words. William Hayman Cummings, organist of Waltham Abbey, fitted it to Wesley’s text in 1855, eight years after Mendelssohn’s death.

“O Come, All Ye Faithful”

The Latin original, Adeste Fideles, is usually credited to John Francis Wade, whose manuscript copies date from the 1740s and whose printed collection of 1751 is its first printed source. Other candidates have been proposed, and the authorship is still disputed. The English translation most churches sing is Frederick Oakeley’s, from 1841.

In “Very God, begotten not created”, very means true, from the Latin verus, and the phrase comes from the Nicene Creed. A fourth-century doctrinal formula sits in the middle of a Christmas carol.

“Lo, He abhors not the Virgin’s womb”, in the same verse, means he does not shrink from being born as a human child.

“In the Bleak Midwinter”

Christina Rossetti’s poem first appeared in January 1872 in Scribner’s Monthly, an American magazine, under the title “A Christmas Carol”. It became a sung carol through two settings: Gustav Holst’s hymn tune Cranham, published in The English Hymnal in 1906, and Harold Darke’s anthem of 1909. “Snow had fallen, snow on snow” does something specific: the repetition slows the line to the pace of the weather it describes.

“Heaven cannot hold Him, nor earth sustain” sets up the contrast the poem turns on, the size of its subject against a stable. In the last verse, “What can I give Him, poor as I am?”, Rossetti turns from the child to the singer and answers her own question with “give my heart”.

The midwinter itself is invention. Nobody knows the season of the birth, and a Judean December looks nothing like the frosty wind Rossetti describes. She was writing about an English winter.

“Once in Royal David’s City”

Cecil Frances Alexander published the words in 1848 in Hymns for Little Children, a collection explaining the Apostles’ Creed to children, which is why the verses are so plainly built. Royal David’s city is Bethlehem, David’s birthplace.

The carol illustrates one clause of the creed, “born of the Virgin Mary”, and “He came down to earth from heaven, who is God and Lord of all” states it outright. The middle verses about the child being “mild, obedient, good as He” are Victorian schoolroom morality and are often cut now, which is a defensible edit.

Since 1919, a year after the first service, the Nine Lessons and Carols at King’s College, Cambridge, has always begun with this carol. In A. H. Mann’s arrangement a chorister sings the first verse alone, and the director of music picks the soloist just before the service starts.

“The Twelve Days of Christmas”

The twelve days run from Christmas Day to Twelfth Night on 5 January, the eve of the Epiphany, not up to Christmas. The song is a cumulative memory game, possibly French in origin, and its words were first printed in London in 1780 in a children’s book, Mirth without Mischief.

You will often be told each gift is a coded catechism for persecuted Catholics. The Canadian hymnologist Hugh McKellar first proposed the idea in 1979 without evidence, and it spread online after 1995. None of the gifts would mark out a Catholic from a Protestant, so leave it out of any introduction from the lectern.

The four calling birds were originally “colly birds”: colly is a dialect word for black, so blackbirds. Frederic Austin’s 1909 arrangement fixed the words most people sing now, calling birds included. Some writers read the five gold rings as ring-necked pheasants, which would make the first seven gifts all birds, but the 1780 book illustrates them as rings.

“Ding Dong! Merrily on High”

The tune is a sixteenth-century French dance, the Branle de l’Official, printed in 1589 in Thoinot Arbeau’s dance manual Orchésographie. The words are much later: George Ratcliffe Woodward published them in 1924 in The Cambridge Carol-Book, deliberately archaic.

“Gloria, Hosanna in excelsis” is the extended refrain everyone recognises, and it means roughly “Glory, save us, in the highest”. Hosanna is Hebrew for “save, we pray”: it began as a plea and became a shout of praise.

Woodward’s verses are full of invented archaism, such as “let steeple bells be swungen”, and were mock-antique when he wrote them. He loved old poetry and wrote pastiche of it.

“Good King Wenceslas”

Wenceslas was a real tenth-century Duke of Bohemia, murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslaus. The Holy Roman Emperor Otto I granted him the title of king after his death, which is why the carol crowns him. The Feast of Stephen is 26 December, so this is a Boxing Day carol rather than a Christmas one.

The story of the page following in his master’s footsteps to keep warm is the point of the whole thing, and the last two lines state the moral outright: “ye who now will bless the poor, shall yourselves find blessing”.

The tune is a thirteenth-century spring carol, Tempus adest floridum, about flowers appearing in the meadows. The tune was first printed in the Finnish collection Piae Cantiones in 1582. J. M. Neale fitted his winter text to it in 1853, in Carols for Christmas-tide with Thomas Helmore. In 1928 the editors of The Oxford Book of Carols counted it among Neale’s weaker pieces, and later critics called the words doggerel.

“Silent Night”

Joseph Mohr wrote the words in 1816, and Franz Xaver Gruber set them for the Christmas Eve Mass at St Nicholas, Oberndorf, in Austria, in 1818. They sang it to a guitar, possibly because flooding had damaged the church organ; the story of a broken organ is not documented. The English translation most churches sing is John Freeman Young’s, published in 1859 from three of Mohr’s six verses.

“Round yon Virgin Mother and Child” puzzles people. Yon means that one over there, so the line means “around that mother and child”. The German has no equivalent: it says that all are asleep and only the holy couple keep watch.

The carol has been translated into about three hundred languages, and Austria entered it on its UNESCO national list of intangible cultural heritage in 2011.

“Deck the Halls”

The tune is Welsh, Nos Galan (New Year’s Eve), printed in 1794 and older than that. The English words are by Thomas Oliphant, published in 1862 in Welsh Melodies, and one of its “fa la la” lines matches an instrumental flourish in the Welsh tune.

In “Troll the ancient Yuletide carol”, to troll a song is to sing it out heartily, or to sing its parts in turn as in a round. “Don we now our gay apparel” is a later line. Oliphant’s was about filling the cup and draining the barrel, and an American school journal printed a version without the drinking in December 1877.

“We Wish You a Merry Christmas”

The words are traditional, from the West Country, and nobody knows how old they are. The version everyone sings was almost unknown outside the West Country until 1935, when the Bristol composer Arthur Warrell arranged it for the University of Bristol Madrigal Singers and Oxford University Press published it.

Figgy pudding is the line people ask about. In the West Country, “figgy” meant made with raisins, so a figgy pudding was a plum pudding, close to a Christmas pudding, and it need not contain a fig. The verses come from the custom of carollers calling at richer houses and expecting food in return, which is why the singers refuse to go until they get some.

Getting the words right on the night

Print every verse you intend to sing, at a size an older congregation can read. People sing the first verse from memory and go quiet in the second when the service sheet is set in ten point.

Decide in advance which verses you are cutting, and tell the choir. If half the congregation sings verse four while the choir has moved to verse five, it takes two lines to recover. For which carols to choose and how to order them, see our guide to the best carols for a carol service.

In the UK a text comes out of copyright seventy years after its author’s death, so every text quoted on this page is free to print. Modern words and translations need a licence; most churches hold a CCLI Church Copyright Licence, so check it covers what you print.

If someone is introducing the carols from the lectern, give them something true to say. A short note about where a text came from works better than the twelve-days catechism story, and nobody has to correct it afterwards.

Frequently asked questions

What does “God rest ye merry, gentlemen” mean?

It means “may God keep you in good spirits, gentlemen”. Rest is the old verb meaning keep, and merry is the state God keeps you in, so the line is a blessing on the gentlemen. The comma belongs after merry, not after rest, and many carol sheets get it wrong.

What does “veiled in flesh the Godhead see” mean?

It is a compressed statement of the incarnation: God hidden inside a human body. The line comes from Charles Wesley’s Hark! The Herald Angels Sing, and the next line, “Hail th’incarnate Deity”, says it more plainly.

When are the twelve days of Christmas?

From Christmas Day to Twelfth Night on 5 January, the eve of the Epiphany, so they follow Christmas rather than lead up to it. The claim that each gift is a coded Catholic catechism has no evidence behind it: a Canadian hymnologist first proposed it in 1979, and it spread online after 1995.

Why is Good King Wenceslas sung at Christmas?

It is set on the Feast of Stephen, 26 December, so it is a Boxing Day carol. Wenceslas was a real tenth-century Duke of Bohemia, and the tune is a thirteenth-century spring carol about flowers, to which J. M. Neale fitted his words in 1853.

What is figgy pudding?

A plum pudding. In the West Country, where We Wish You a Merry Christmas comes from, “figgy” meant made with raisins, so a figgy pudding was close to a Christmas pudding and need not contain figs. The carollers demand it because the song comes from the custom of singing at richer houses for food.

Where can I find the full words to Christmas carols?

Hymnary.org prints the full texts of most traditional carols, and hymn books such as The New English Hymnal carry the versions churches sing. In the UK a text is out of copyright seventy years after its author’s death; modern words and translations need a licence, such as the CCLI Church Copyright Licence, before you print them.
